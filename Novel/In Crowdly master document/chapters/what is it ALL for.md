# What is it ALL for?

I went home from the appointment with the doctor's words in my bag. She had written down what she wanted me to do next, but I left the paper folded until Eduard came in. I told him about the pain, the tests, and the possibility she had raised. I had rehearsed the conversation on the way home; he was the first person to hear it, and I still lost my place.

He read the paper and sat beside me.

“What happens next?” he asked.

“More tests. A surgeon. I don't know.”

“We'll find out together.”

I wanted to tell him I did not trust the people who had given me this news. Since Villete, a doctor's certainty could bring back the memory of being told my heart was failing. I also wanted the doctor to be certain this time, so long as she could tell me I was well. I held those wishes together without knowing what to do with either one.

That evening Luka came to the table with a drawing of our house. He had given Eduard a studio twice the size of the house itself. Zala noticed the mistake and insisted on adding her own room with a door no one else could use. They argued about the roof until Eduard found another sheet of paper. I laughed with them. Then I looked down at the folded note by my plate and felt a fear so sudden I had to excuse myself.

I had imagined dying before. In Villete I had once wanted to. Years later, with two children downstairs and a husband asking whether I wanted tea, the prospect felt entirely different. I wanted more mornings. I wanted to see what Zala would do when she was old enough to leave us and what Luka would choose to draw when no one asked him to draw home. Wanting those things did not make me brave. It made me afraid of losing them.

I went to the appointments. At one of them, a doctor spoke as though cancer had already become the name for what they had found. Another wanted the results checked before any treatment was decided. I asked for copies of everything and carried them from one room to the next. Eduard came when I asked him to. When I wanted to speak for myself, he let me. When I forgot a question, he remembered it.

For a while our days divided themselves into before and after each telephone call. I could work between them, feed the children, answer messages, and appear almost unchanged. Then a call came, or failed to come, and everything else lost its shape. The children noticed more than we told them. Luka asked whether I was going away again. Zala asked why I was sitting still when my bag was packed for work.

One morning, while the house was quiet, I tried the breathing exercise I had learned in Andalusia. I could count each breath. I could feel my feet on the floor. I could also think, between one breath and the next, that I might not be there for Zala's next birthday. The exercise helped me stay in the room long enough to notice the thought. It could not make the thought untrue or decide what the tests meant.

When the doctors finished reviewing the results, they told us the earlier conclusion had been wrong. I did not have the cancer they had feared. I asked the doctor to explain it again, then asked what follow-up I needed for the pain that had brought me in. I wrote down the answer. On the way home Eduard kept one hand on the steering wheel and the other near mine.

“Are you relieved?” he asked.

“Yes.”

He glanced at me. “You don't sound it.”

“I am. I don't know what to do with it yet.”

At home I hugged the children too long. Zala wriggled free. Luka let me hold him a moment more, then asked if he could finish his drawing. By the next day our calendar looked ordinary again. I had expected relief to restore everything instantly. It did not. I was grateful and exhausted, angry about the weeks of fear, and embarrassed by that anger when I knew other people were receiving much worse news.

My work had not stopped. We had footage to review and plans for another series. People wrote to say they liked a programme I had made months earlier. I read their messages, answered a few, and wondered when the pleasure I used to take in that exchange had become so brief. I was making more programmes, going farther, and coming home with less I could tell my family that I had truly noticed. Sometimes I had to look at my notes to remember the name of a person whose story I had asked them to trust me with.

I went into the garden and found the pots I had planted after Spain. Some had flourished. Others were dry because I had left them to Eduard without saying what they needed. I watered them, although one was already beyond saving. An invitation to travel lay on the kitchen table. It interested me. So did the sudden prospect of saying no.

What was all this for? I asked myself while I washed soil from my hands. I had once answered that question simply: to stay alive long enough to see what life could become. I had seen more than I could have imagined in Villete. I loved Eduard and the children. I liked the work I knew how to do. Yet I had begun to arrange my days so tightly that I could move through them without asking whether I was present for any of it.

I did not think I had wasted my life. I could not bear the tidy story in which a frightening diagnosis taught me, in a single afternoon, how to live correctly. The fear had gone, and my habits were still here. I reached for the next assignment before I had decided why I wanted it. On days when I stayed home, I could feel an old heaviness waiting beneath the activity. Breathing slowly helped me notice it; it did not always lift it. I told Eduard as much, and we talked about getting help if it continued. I wanted to take my unease seriously without allowing it to speak for every part of my life.

A producer called about a journey through Southeast Asia. There would be filming in Thailand, with further travel if the work went well. I said I wanted to see the plan before committing. That was new for me.

At dinner I told Eduard and the children. Luka asked whether there were trains. Zala wanted to know if she could come, then whether I would bring her a story if she could not. Eduard waited until they had left the table.

“Do you want this trip?”

“I think so. I want to do the work. And afterwards I'd like to stay a little longer.”

“To find something?”

“I don't know. Perhaps to have enough time somewhere that I stop asking what comes next.”

He looked at me for a while. “Then make sure there is enough time.”

We went through the calendar together. I kept the appointments the doctors had advised. The children knew when I would leave and when I expected to return. I agreed to the assignment and booked extra days after the crew's schedule ended. For once I told them those days were mine before someone filled them.

In December, I closed my suitcase and checked the small notebook from Spain. The breathing instructions were still there beside my attempts at Spanish. I took it with me, although I knew I could practise without reading the page. At the airport I called home. Luka reported that he had found a book about trains in Thailand. Zala said goodnight even though it was morning where I stood.

I boarded with no answer grand enough to fit the question. I did know that I wanted to come back to them, and that while I was away I wanted to pay attention to where I was.
