# Hello Great Britain

For weeks after we returned from India, Eduard woke whenever he smelled smoke. The fire had followed us home in smaller ways. I checked the door to Luka's room before I went to bed. He checked it after me.

Bern was safe, orderly, and suddenly difficult for me to breathe in. I had wanted to come home when we were in Goa. Once I was home, I kept looking at the closed suitcase and thinking about where it might go next. I was pregnant again. We would soon have two children. Leaving a place merely because I felt restless seemed irresponsible, which did not make the feeling disappear.

One evening Eduard came in from the studio with a letter from a gallery in Britain. I was at the kitchen table, cutting an apple for Luka.

“I could send the paintings,” he said. “They've asked me to come as well.”

“Then go.”

“For a week, perhaps. I'd like to see what else is there.”

I put a piece of apple on Luka's plate. “So would I.”

Eduard pulled out a chair. He had meant the galleries, I think. I was already imagining bookshops, newspapers, people to whom I could send my work. We began talking about a visit and ended up talking about a house. Neither of us could later say who first suggested moving. By the time Luka was asleep, we were making a list much like the one I had made for India.

I had been writing steadily since his birth. I had pages about motherhood that made me laugh when I reread them and others I could barely bear to show anyone. I wanted to publish them, and I wanted to find out whether I could write something larger than the life immediately around me. Eduard wanted to meet people who would look at his paintings without first asking his parents what they thought. Britain offered us each a way to try. We knew that trying would be easier with money behind us; his work had sold well enough that we could afford the move and afford to be wrong.

In March 2000, we took Luka to our new home in Britain. I was carrying his younger sibling and still getting used to calling the baby *she*. The rooms were unfamiliar, the light was different, and the first delivery arrived while we were still deciding where anything belonged. Luka took his first steps between two unopened boxes. Eduard painted when he could. I wrote at the kitchen table and sent pages out while Luka slept.

I had expected the move to make me feel instantly free. Instead I spent the first weeks learning the routes to the shops, finding someone to call when Luka was ill, and asking people to repeat themselves when I missed the end of a sentence. I could speak English; I did not yet know all the ways people used it to be polite, funny, or unkind. I liked listening. By the time I understood a joke well enough to answer, the conversation had usually moved on.

On 5 August, Zala was born.

Her hair was dark, a surprise after Luka's fair head. Eduard said she had my eyes. I could not tell. I was looking for signs that she was comfortable, that she was feeding, that we were doing what she needed. When Luka arrived, his sleep had made us look more competent than we were. Zala had no interest in protecting our reputation.

She cried in the evenings. She cried when I laid her down and sometimes when I picked her up. There were nights when feeding helped, nights when walking helped, and nights when nothing helped for long. We took turns carrying her through the house. Luka slept through most of it, which felt both merciful and faintly insulting.

At three in the morning I stood by the bedroom window with Zala against my shoulder. A few lights were still on across the street. I wondered if every household behind them had its own reason to be awake. My daughter gave a sharp little cry, then rested her cheek against my neck. I loved her so fiercely that I wanted to know what was hurting her. I was also angry, exhausted, and close to tears because I wanted to put her down. Both things were true. I had learned that with Luka, but Zala made me learn it again.

Eduard came up behind us and held out his arms.

“She'll wake you,” I whispered.

“She already has.”

I passed her to him. He began to walk the length of the room, speaking softly about the paintings stacked in his studio and the strange shapes people said they could see in them. Zala kept crying. I lay down anyway. For a few minutes I listened to his voice and let him be the parent who knew what to do next, even if all he knew was how to keep walking.

The following afternoon, when both children slept at once, I wrote down that night. The page was untidy and much too honest. I kept it. Over the next months I wrote more about our house, our children, my impatience, and the advice strangers offered me as though I had never met a baby before. I found I could make other mothers laugh by admitting what had made me feel ashamed. Some of the pieces were published. Readers wrote back, often with their own stories, and I began to understand that my private confusion was crowded with company.

The work grew. I was asked for longer pieces, then a book. When I went to speak about it, someone suggested I might be good on television. I was flattered and certain they had mistaken confidence for ability. My first time in a studio, I talked too quickly and forgot one of the questions I had prepared. Afterwards I sat in the car with my hands on the steering wheel, replaying every sentence. The producer called to ask whether I would come back.

I did. I learned to look into a camera without waiting for it to look back. I liked meeting people, asking how they lived, and finding the detail that made a place more than a name on a map. A short segment became another assignment. I began to see how the writing and the television work could belong to the same life, though they demanded different kinds of attention. On a page I could stop and reconsider. On air, the next sentence had to come while people were listening.

Eduard's work reached new galleries too. His paintings travelled farther than either of us did for a while, and then he travelled with them. We stopped counting each sale as a surprise. We had more money than we had expected when we left Ljubljana, and it made many difficult things easier. It could pay for help when we needed it and buy me time to work. It could not make Zala sleep or decide for us which parent would leave next and which would stay.

We tried arrangements and changed them when they failed. Sometimes I stayed home while Eduard went to an opening. Sometimes he took the children while I travelled for a story. I could tell myself that the work mattered, and it did. I could also see the disappointment on Luka's face when I missed a promised afternoon. Once, when I returned from filming, he pointed at the television and then at me, delighted that I existed in both places. I laughed with him. Later I sat alone with the thought that he had become accustomed to seeing me on a screen.

Zala, by then, had learned to say “no” with a force that made the word sound like a complete philosophy. She wanted to climb before she could safely get down. Luka watched her experiments from the floor and offered her a toy when she fell. Their differences filled the house more completely than any furniture we bought.

By the spring of 2004, our life in Britain had become familiar enough that I could leave it without wondering whether it would still be there when I returned. I had an assignment in Spain. On the night before my flight, I finished packing after the children were in bed and found Eduard in the studio.

“Will you see anything besides the places on your schedule?” he asked.

“I intend to.”

“That wasn't on the schedule.”

“That's the point.”

He smiled, then asked me to call when I arrived. He had been asking since India. I promised that I would.
