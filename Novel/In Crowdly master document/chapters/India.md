# India

November 1999. New Delhi.

Luka fell asleep during our descent and woke as soon as the plane stopped. I carried him through the airport with his cheek against mine, trying to keep track of our bags and the place where we would find a car. By the time we reached our room, he was ready to play. I sat on the bed without taking off my shoes.

This was the journey I had argued for, planned, and wanted. I had imagined the moment of arrival many times. In none of those imaginings was I searching the bottom of a bag for a clean cloth while my son tried to pull the telephone cord from the wall.

I called Eduard. He asked first about Luka, then about me.

“We're here,” I said. “That's all I can tell you so far.”

“It sounds like enough for one day.”

He told me the river had risen after rain. I could picture the view from his studio more clearly than I could picture the street outside my window. When we hung up, I fed Luka and lay down beside him. For a while I listened to unfamiliar traffic and missed a house I had been impatient to leave.

In the morning I took us outside.

At first I made plans as if I were travelling alone: a long walk before breakfast, a museum in the afternoon, a train the next day. Luka revised them. He needed food when I wanted to leave, sleep when I wanted to look around, and a change of clothes at precisely the least convenient moment. Once I stopped treating his needs as delays, I began to notice what our slower pace gave me. I could sit in a courtyard and stay until he woke. I could return to the same small restaurant and be greeted by someone who remembered us. I learned which bag to open first before he cried.

We went from Delhi to Agra and Lucknow, then south by stages through Mumbai, Chennai, and Puducherry. There were days when I wrote pages in a notebook after Luka fell asleep and days when I wrote only a place name and the hour of his next feed. In photographs I looked cheerful, usually because the camera came out after I had found what we needed. I sent some to Eduard anyway. I wanted him to see Luka changing and to know where we were, though each new place made our days harder to describe over the telephone.

At the coast I kept a respectful distance from the water.

When I was a child, I had nearly drowned. Someone pulled me out in time; I learned to swim later. Neither fact erased the moment when I could not find the surface. An older woman once told me that I would die by drowning. I had no reason to believe she knew the future, but her words had found a place beside my memory and stayed there.

I carried Luka towards the sea until the sand grew firm beneath my feet. The waves reached us and withdrew. He leaned forward to watch the water and laughed when it touched my ankles. I stepped back before the next wave came.

“You're braver than I am,” I told him.

He reached for it again. I held him securely and let the water come once more.

By the time we reached Varkala, I had learned how to travel with him, though I was still tired most evenings. I went to a yoga session where mothers were welcome to bring their children. Some women followed every movement with closed eyes. Others paused to feed a baby or retrieve one crawling towards the door. I belonged easily to the second group.

A man I had seen near the place where we stayed spoke to me afterwards. He was courteous, almost carefully so, and asked whether Luka had enjoyed the morning. Over the next few days our paths crossed again. He lent me a book. I gave him one I had finished. When he invited me to lunch, I went.

Our table looked out towards the sea. Luka wanted to be held, so I ate with one hand and fed him when he became impatient. The man waited while I settled him, then picked up our conversation where we had left it. He asked about my writing. I told him about Bern, about Eduard's paintings, and about the trip. He told me about his work and the places he had lived. It was pleasant to speak as myself for an hour, rather than answer another stranger's questions about travelling with a baby.

I knew he was interested in me. I was interested in him too. Marriage had not made me incapable of noticing another person, and being a mother had not made me incapable of wanting to be noticed. I had not decided what, if anything, I would do with that feeling.

The next time we met, he walked Luka and me back to our room. Luka was asleep by then. I invited the man in, put my son in his cot, and turned to find him waiting a little way behind me. He kissed me. I kissed him back.

Then my stomach lurched.

I barely reached the bathroom. When I came out, still holding a damp cloth to my mouth, he was standing by the door.

“Are you all right?” he asked.

“I don't know.”

He looked towards Luka, who was still asleep. “Do you want me to get someone?”

“No. Thank you.”

He left quietly. I appreciated that he did not ask for an explanation I couldn't give. I sat on the floor until the room stopped moving, then began counting backwards: days, weeks, the evening before our flight, Eduard's studio floor.

“Oh, fuck,” I said.

Luka stirred, and I went to him.

I could not know for certain yet. Even so, the possibility had arrived so fully that the room seemed smaller around it. I pictured another child where, until that evening, I had seen only the two of us travelling. I loved Luka. Some days caring for him took all the strength I had. The thought of beginning again so soon frightened me.

The next morning I arranged to leave for Kochi. In the vehicle to the station Luka pressed a hand against the window and watched the streets pass. I watched him, then looked away. I considered ending the pregnancy. I considered keeping it. I did not know what Eduard would think, or even what I wanted once the fear settled. Being far from him made every possible future feel like a decision I had to make alone.

At Villete I had once tried to end my own life. Now I found myself asking what responsibility I had towards a life that might be beginning inside me. That memory mattered to me, though it did not give me a simple rule. I was tired of pretending that a difficult choice became easy because someone had found a sentence that sounded certain.

On the train I held Luka while he slept. I wanted time to find out what was true before deciding anything. When we stopped, I called Eduard.

“I think I'm pregnant,” I said.

For a moment I heard only the line between us.

“Are you sure?”

“No.”

“Are you all right?”

“I'm afraid.”

He did not tell me how to feel. We talked until I could say aloud what had happened and what I was considering. He listened. When I finally asked him to come, he said he would arrange it.

By the time I saw him again, I had confirmed the pregnancy. I had also decided I wanted to continue it. The decision had taken days of thinking, some of it with Eduard on the telephone and much of it while I cared for Luka. I was relieved to see him, and angry that he could arrive after the hardest part and still make me feel safer simply by standing there.

He took our son from me. Luka stared at him for a second before grabbing his collar. Eduard laughed, and I cried. Later, when Luka slept, we spoke about the new baby. Eduard put his hand over mine on the table. Neither of us claimed to know what our lives would be like with two children.

We went on to Goa together, first to Agonda and then to Arambol. It was a gentler journey with another pair of hands. Eduard carried Luka down to the water and stopped where the sand was wet. I joined them but stayed well clear of the breaking waves. He did not urge me farther. For a few days our only plans were where to eat and whether Luka needed sleep. I began to look forward to going home.

## Fire

On our last night, Eduard had nearly finished packing. The suitcases stood closed beside the bed; Luka slept in his cot. I woke because Eduard touched my shoulder.

“Veronika. Get up.”

His voice was quiet enough that I opened my eyes without understanding him. The ceiling above us was lit orange. Smoke was gathering near it.

I reached for Luka. Eduard took my free hand and led us outside. The air there felt cool for one breath. Then I heard shouting from another room and turned to see people coming out of their doors.

“Keep going,” Eduard said. “Away from the building.”

He went back to wake the guests who were still inside. I wanted to call him back. I could not leave Luka alone, and I could not carry him towards the smoke. I stood with him at a distance, watching the entrance. Other people joined us, some dressed, some barefoot, all turning to count whoever had come with them.

Eduard appeared with a bag and set it down. He called to a person still at an upstairs window and went towards the building again. This time, when he came out, he was coughing. The fire had grown too strong for anyone to return. Our stroller was still inside.

“It doesn't matter,” I said when he told me.

“I know.”

He looked at Luka. I could see him counting the three of us, the same way I had been counting since the moment we ran out.

We stayed awake until morning. By then the room where we had slept was damaged beyond anything we could collect from it. We took what we had and made our way to the airport. On the journey north, Luka slept against Eduard while I watched the ground fall away below us.

In Bern, people would ask what India had been like. I had notebooks, photographs, and stories enough to answer for hours. For the moment I wanted to get our son into his own bed and tell Eduard that I was glad he had come.
