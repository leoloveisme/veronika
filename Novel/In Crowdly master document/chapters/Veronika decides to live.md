# Veronika decides to live

The last part of the journey was made possible by people whose names I would
not put in my notebook. Matej and I entered Gaza in August 2025 with our
equipment reduced to what we could carry and with no assurance that the way out
would still be open when we needed it. I had asked those who helped us whether
our presence was worth the trouble it created. One woman who worked there
answered that we should listen first and decide what to film after. I tried to
remember her instruction each time I raised the camera.

I had imagined my return to work after the snakebite as a test of strength. The
heat settled that idea quickly. I tired sooner than I wanted to admit. Matej
noticed and waited for me without commenting. We spoke with people who were
trying to keep a clinic open, people searching for relatives, parents who had
learned to measure a day by whether their children had eaten. I did not ask
them what lesson they wished the world to take from their suffering. I asked
what they wanted to say. I checked the names we wrote down. We did not film
faces when someone asked us not to.

One afternoon we finished an interview near a street of damaged buildings.
Matej turned to pack the equipment. A woman who had been helping us translate
was called away. I saw children at the far end of the street, standing close
together and looking towards a building where other people were moving. I told
Matej I was going to see whether they needed help. He nodded without looking
up. Later he would remember hearing my voice and wish he had caught the words.

There were five of them. The oldest, Noor, told me their names as I crouched
beside them. She was eight. Jamal and Amal were twins of six; Amal kept
watching the sky. Omar, five, held the hand of his little sister Yasmin. Yasmin
was three and held a stuffed animal by one ear. I repeated the names until I
had them right. I had learned some Arabic, but I needed Noor’s patience to
understand where they had been told to go.

“Is your family there?” I asked, pointing towards the people at the building.

Noor nodded and pointed farther along the street. She was trying to get her
younger siblings across the open space. A sound overhead made Amal duck. I
looked up and saw a drone, small against the glare. I had heard aircraft before
that day; I could not tell what this one would do. We needed to leave the
street.

I took Yasmin’s free hand. Dust had settled on her cheek, and I wiped it away
with my thumb. She looked at me as if I had delayed us for no reason.

“We’ll go together,” I said. “On three.”

I counted in Arabic, slowly enough for the smallest children to follow. Noor
answered me and began to lead. Omar would not let go of Yasmin. Amal took
Jamal’s hand. For a few steps we moved as a single, awkward line.

The blast came from a building ahead of us. I saw part of its wall break and
felt the ground move beneath my feet. I pulled Yasmin towards me. There was
another noise, larger than any word I could have shouted, and the air filled
with pieces of the street. I lost her hand.

I cannot tell what each child saw in the last moment. I know their names. I
know that they were trying to reach people they trusted. I had come to make a
film, and for those few steps the only thing I wanted was to get them across
the road.

Matej found the place after the dust began to settle. Someone held him back
while others searched the fallen masonry. The five children had been killed.
Veronika had too. He could not make himself tell Eduard that in a message, so
he called. He said the children’s names first, because I had written them on a
loose page he found in my notebook. Then he told him mine.

Eduard asked him to say it again. Matej did. Ana was with Eduard when he put
down the telephone. Luka and Zala came as soon as they could. There were
arrangements to make and questions that remained unanswered. They did what was
necessary, sometimes together and sometimes in separate rooms. None of them
called my death the fulfilment of my life. They knew I had wanted to come home.

Matej preserved the footage and the names in the notebook. The people who had
helped us decide what could be shown and what should remain with the families.
The film, in the form we had planned, was never finished. I had left other work
behind: books, programmes, paintings I had argued about with Eduard, a garden
that needed tending. Those things could not answer for my absence. They
remained because I had made them while I was alive.

In January 2026, a sixteen-year-old girl called Veronika was admitted to a
hospital in Ljubljana after jumping from a window to escape an attempt to abuse
her. The fall had broken her leg and bruised her shoulder and back. She had
more questions from adults than she could bear to answer. Her parents, who had
come to Slovenia from Ukraine, visited whenever they were allowed. She loved
them and could not find a way to tell them that she was thinking of ending her
life after she left the hospital. She had survived the fall; survival did not
yet feel like safety.

The older woman in the next bed was called Marija. She was waiting for an
operation on her left shoulder. On the first day she asked the girl’s name.

“Veronika.”

“That was my mother’s name,” Marija said. “I’m Marija.”

The girl turned towards the wall. Marija let her. She knitted for a while, put
her work away, and made no further attempt to draw out a conversation.

The next day Marija’s daughter brought clean clothes, food and two books.
Marija read one of them until the light began to fade. Then she set the other
on the shared nightstand. Its cover faced the girl’s bed: *Veronika Decides to
Live.* The younger Veronika saw her name and looked away.

That night she could not sleep. She reached for the book, drew her hand back,
then picked it up. The story was another Veronika, a woman who had once woken
in a Ljubljana hospital furious to find herself alive. The girl read several
pages and stopped. She did not like the older woman immediately. Some sentences
sounded as if they had been written by someone with more choices than she had
ever been offered. Others made her want to keep reading.

“You can borrow it,” Marija murmured from the other bed.

“I wasn’t—”

“It’s all right. Keep it as long as you want.”

Marija went back to sleep. The girl read until the words blurred. In the days
that followed, she spoke a little more. She learned why Marija was in hospital,
which food her daughter would bring, and how to ask a nurse for help without
apologising first. Marija did not tell her that a book would fix her life.

When Marija was discharged, she paused at the door. “I’m glad I met you,
Veronika.”

The girl held the book in her lap. After Marija left, she finished it. She
reached the account of Noor, Jamal, Amal, Omar and Yasmin, and the page ended
before any of them could be led to safety. The girl closed the cover and stared
at the name printed there. She was angry with the woman who had gone into the
street. She was angry that the children had died. None of the earlier chapters
could make that ending fair.

“You foolish woman,” she whispered. After a while she added, “But you tried.”

Outside the window, Ljubljana was grey and busy with people going about a
winter day. The view did not change when she put the book down. She thought of
what awaited her after discharge and felt afraid. Then she pressed the call
button and told the nurse who came that she needed help, that she had been
thinking of killing herself. The nurse pulled up a chair. Veronika began again
when her first words would not come out clearly.

Years later, in 2040, she walked a mountain path above the sea with her husband
and their two children. The younger child stopped to examine a stone. Everyone
waited. Wind came up from the water, and one of the children complained that
they had brought the wrong coats. Veronika laughed and opened the bag to see
what she had packed. There was food enough to share before they went on. She
had no final answer to the question of how to live. They chose a place to sit,
called the children back from the edge of the path, and stayed for a while in
the ordinary day they had been given.

And in the end, the machine took me too.
