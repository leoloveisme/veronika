# Latin America

I crossed into Mexico with a suitcase full of clothes I had chosen for the United States. They were wrong for the heat, and I was tired of opening that suitcase in a new room every few nights. During the American assignment, the crew and I had travelled enormous distances and brought home the footage to prove it. When the work ended, I could still hear the rhythm of the next appointment in my head.

This time I was travelling without a crew. Nobody was expecting a report from me by evening. I had told Eduard I would spend some time in Mexico before coming home, and he had asked me to decide what *some time* meant once I arrived. I promised to call the children. I did not promise an itinerary I knew I would change.

In Mexico City I spent the first morning doing almost nothing. I sat over breakfast long after I had finished eating and watched people pass the window. I could have found a story in every conversation I overheard. I took out my notebook, wrote down the date, and put it away again. It felt strange to let a day happen without collecting it for someone else.

My Spanish was better than it had been in Spain, though it still deserted me when I wanted to say something complicated. That made me listen. A woman at a bookshop recommended a place to eat because I had asked about a novel on display. At the restaurant I stayed through a rain shower and spoke with a family at the next table about children, work, and the cities they had lived in. I told them about Luka and Zala. For once I did not try to turn the exchange into an interview.

I travelled on, sometimes deciding only the next stop. In Oaxaca I spent several days walking familiar streets until a seller at the market remembered which fruit I liked. I sent Eduard a photograph of a courtyard where I wished I could plant something. He called that night.

“You can still come home and plant it here,” he said.

“I know.”

“Do you want to?”

“Of course. I also want to stay a little longer.”

He was quiet for a moment. “I thought you might.”

The children took turns with the telephone. Luka wanted to know what I had eaten. Zala asked whether I had met anyone famous. I told her I had met people I would remember, which she considered a way of avoiding the question. She was right. When the call ended, I missed them sharply. Missing them did not make me book a flight.

In Iceland I had gone into the ocean to see what fear became when no one watched. In the United States I had moved so quickly that I could hardly hear myself think. Here, with a slower day around me, thoughts arrived that I had been able to outrun elsewhere. Why did I need the next country? What did I hope it would give me that I could not ask for at home? I had no useful answer yet.

Someone I knew through my journalism put me in touch with people farther south who organised visits to an ayahuasca ceremony in the Amazon. I had heard of it before. My first impulse was to use my name and money to arrange the experience quickly, as though access and understanding were the same thing. The woman helping me asked why I wanted to go. I gave her three answers that sounded good. She waited. Eventually I admitted that I was curious and that I hoped to learn something about the question I kept carrying from place to place.

She did not promise that I would. I liked her better for it.

I travelled to Peru and then towards the forest. Along the way I found myself trying to describe the journey to Eduard. I stopped after writing two pages of scenery and started again with the simpler truth: *I don't know what I'm looking for, but I am being careful about whom I trust.* I told him where I was staying and when I expected to call. He replied that Luka had borrowed one of my notebooks and was using it to draw machines no one had yet invented.

The people who received us explained what they were offering and what they expected from visitors. I asked questions, including some that made me feel ignorant. They answered what they could and told me when something was not mine to take away and explain to a television audience. I left my camera behind. This was their work and their place; I had paid to be there, and that did not make it mine.

Before the ceremony I imagined I would meet a version of myself who knew, at last, what to do with the rest of her life. During it, I lost hold of that tidy expectation. Time seemed to lengthen and fold back on itself. I remembered the fire in Goa with such force that I looked for smoke. I saw Zala as a baby, red-faced and furious in my arms, then as she had been on our last telephone call, demanding an honest answer. I heard water and could not tell for a moment whether it belonged to the room, to Iceland, or to the childhood lake I still carried inside me.

Some of what I felt frightened me. Some of it was beautiful. Much of it was confused. People were nearby, and I was grateful for that, but the experience belonged to no one else in quite the form it took for me. I did not receive a sentence to live by. By morning I was tired and unwilling to turn the night into a revelation merely because I had travelled so far for it.

I stayed for the conversations afterwards. I listened to the people who had led the ceremony and to the other visitors, whose accounts differed from mine. One person spoke with certainty about what had happened to him. I envied that certainty and did not share it. When someone asked what I had learned, I said I needed time before I could answer.

I spent that time travelling back through the places I had crossed too quickly. I wrote more slowly. I called home when I said I would. I noticed that in telling Eduard about a day, I wanted to tell him what a person had said rather than how many places I had seen. Once I tried to explain the ceremony and stopped halfway through.

“I can't make it sound true,” I said.

“Then tell me the part you can.”

“I thought I'd find an answer there. I didn't.”

He let me sit with that for a moment. Then he told me Zala had planted seeds in the wrong season because she disliked being told to wait. I laughed. I could see our kitchen, his paint-stained hands, and our daughter guarding a pot of soil as if it contained a secret.

When I finally returned to Britain, I brought gifts for the children and a notebook for myself. I did not come home quieter in every way. The journeys still excited me, and I still wanted to leave again someday. But I had begun to hear the difference between going somewhere because I was interested in it and going because I could not bear the space left by staying.

Eduard met me at the airport. He took the suitcase and kissed me while people moved around us. I told him I had missed him. He said he knew. When we reached home, the children wanted the gifts first and my stories second. That was fair. There would be time for both.
