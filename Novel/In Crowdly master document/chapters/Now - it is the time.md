# Now - it’s the time

I woke in the afternoon. I knew it was afternoon because the light reached the
wall above the bed instead of falling across my face. I did not know where the
wall was. When I tried to turn my head, the room seemed to move before I did.

Someone said my name. The voice was close and familiar, but I could not place
it among the small noises around me. A monitor sounded; a door opened somewhere
behind my bed; a wheel passed along the corridor. I wanted all of it to stop. I
closed my eyes and waited for the noise to separate into things I could
recognise.

When I opened them again, Eduard was there.

“Ljubljana,” he said. “You’re in Ljubljana.”

I looked at him for a long time. His face was thinner than I remembered. He had
made it across the world with me, and I had missed the journey. I tried to ask
how long I had been gone. The words did not come out clearly. He understood
enough to tell me that it was August. I had been brought to Ljubljana in May,
and the doctors had been looking after me through the months I could not
remember. He put his hand near mine and waited until I could close my fingers around
it.

I had hated hospitals since Villette. Once, waking in a bed in this city had
felt like a sentence: I was still alive and could see no reason to be glad.
This room brought some of the old fear back. I could not decide when to get up
or even whether the light should stay on. But when a nurse came to check on me,
I watched her work with such relief that I almost cried. I was alive because
people had done things for me while I could do nothing for myself.

I wanted to tell Eduard what I remembered. It was not an orderly journey
through the days I had lost. There had been moments of discomfort, or perhaps
dreams of discomfort. Light seemed to arrive all at once. Sounds grew too
large. At other times I had felt as though the edges of my body were gone. I
cannot say which of those sensations belonged to the coma and which came as I
began to wake. I only know that for a while I did not feel confined to the
person named Veronika. There was space, and a quiet I had spent years crossing
oceans to find.

I called it peace when I could speak again. Once, half asleep, I called it
nirvana. Those were names I gave the experience afterwards. The doctors could
tell me what had happened to my body; they could not tell me what it had meant
to be inside it. Nor could I offer my private feeling as a lesson anyone else
ought to learn from nearly dying.

The first morning I sat up for longer than a few minutes, I grew tired before I
finished breakfast. I had imagined recovery as the glorious moment when a
person opens her eyes. Instead it was a succession of small tasks. Drink. Sit.
Stand with help. Find the bathroom. Walk to the window and back. Some days I
could do less than I had done the day before. I had to learn that this did not
erase the earlier day.

Eduard brought my bag from India. He found my bracelet in a pocket and held it
out to me. I remembered someone removing it at the clinic. I remembered
reaching for a branch at the farm, then the marks on my leg. I did not remember
the flight to Slovenia. He told me about it in pieces, allowing me to stop him
when I had heard enough.

“You were there all that time?” I asked.

“As much as they let me be.”

“And the children?”

“They came. They’re coming again.”

I looked at his hands. He had not been painting much. I had spent years saying
I did not fear death, as if that settled what dying would mean for him. I could
see now that he had lived through days I had left him to endure without an
answer.

Zala arrived from Colombia with a story already in progress. She had wanted to
tell me about one of her students, she said, but I had not answered her the
last time. She stopped when she heard herself. We sat together until she was
ready to begin again. Luka came with the notebook he had rescued from the
crew’s things. He had kept it closed. He asked whether I wanted it, and I said
not yet. He put it where I could reach it when I changed my mind.

Matej visited too. For a while we spoke only about the film we had abandoned.
The footage was safe. The people who had trusted us with their stories would
need to know what had happened to the project. We agreed that we could decide
what to do when I was well enough to see it, and with their permission where it
was needed. Then he told me how frightened he had been at the farm. I could
hear him trying to find the moment in which he might have stopped me.

“You told me to leave the snake alone,” I said. “I made my own choice.”

“I wish you hadn’t.”

“So do I.”

It was easier to say that than to give the accident a grand meaning. I had not
been chosen for a revelation. I had made a mistake, become very ill, and
survived because others acted quickly and continued to care for me. The peace I
remembered was real to me; so were the people who had worked through the hours
I could not remember.

When I left the hospital, Eduard and I stayed in Ljubljana. He had begun to
look for land in Slovenia before I went to India. Now even a short walk beside
the river took more effort than I expected. I did not want him to mistake my
need to rest for a promise to retire. He did not ask for one. He brought chairs
outside when the weather was good and let me decide when I had sat long enough.

The children had come and gone through the summer while I lay unconscious.
Zala had returned to her teaching in Colombia, then came back when I woke. Luka spoke about his work and the possibility of taking it to the
United States, still a possibility, not a departure date. They were old enough
to be frightened for me and to have lives that went on while I recovered. I
wanted to know those lives. I tried to ask questions without treating every
visit as a reassurance I needed them to provide.

Ana visited one afternoon when Eduard was out. She brought food and put it away
before sitting with me. The four of us had not found an easy name for what had
grown between us and Matej. In the hospital there had been no time to discuss
it. Now there was time, and the ordinary awkwardness of being in the same room
without an emergency to organise us.

“He called me every day from India,” she said of Matej. “Even when all he could
say was that you were still there.”

I thanked her for driving Eduard to the airport. She said she would have done
it for any of us. Then we ate the food she had brought and talked about
something else. There was affection between us, though it had not removed
everything difficult. I was glad she had come.

I thought about swimming, but even a walk beside the river exhausted me.
Eduard and I sat in the shade, and I let the water pass without entering it.
I did not need the cold to prove I was alive. When we went home, we ate lunch.
The rest of the day had no great event in it, and I enjoyed it.

For the days that followed I thought that might be the change I had brought back: I could
let an ordinary day remain ordinary. I had once filled every quiet moment with
a plan to go somewhere. Now I could sit beside Eduard while he drew and feel no
accusation in the empty page before me. Sometimes I still wanted to travel. I
was beginning to see that wanting to stay and wanting to go could both be
honest.

Later that August my mother called. I was at the kitchen table with the window
open. She asked how I felt, then told me she had read that Dr. Igor had died of
a heart attack.

I said his name back to her. She knew I had heard and let the silence stand
between us. He had been the psychiatrist at Villette who told me my heart was
failing and that I had little time left. Later I learned he had lied. The fear
his words gave me had sent me into the world, though that did not make the lie
kind or its consequences simple. He had been part of the beginning of this life
I loved, and now he was dead.

At his funeral I stood with Eduard, Luka and Zala. Zala asked quietly who he
had been to us.

“Someone who changed my life,” I said. “Not in a way I would ask anyone to
repeat.”

She took my hand. I looked at the flowers, at the people who had known him in
ways I had not, and remembered being young enough to believe that one man could
tell me exactly how much life remained. I had no such certainty now. I was
grateful for it.

Not long after the funeral, a producer contacted me about a documentary in
Gaza. I had followed the reports since waking: families displaced,
hospitals under strain, children going without enough food. The scale of what
people were living through troubled me. It did not make me uniquely qualified
to tell their story. I asked whom we would work with, how local colleagues
would be involved, and whether our presence would help anyone understand what
was happening rather than add another camera to a place already crowded with
them.

The answers took time. Access was uncertain and safety could not be promised.
Matej and I spoke with people who knew the work there, and with one another. I
told Eduard before I accepted. He had just spent weeks waiting for me to wake,
and I could see him measuring the distance from our kitchen to another hospital
bed, or worse.

“I don’t know how to ask you not to go,” he said.

“You can ask.”

“Would it change your mind?”

I looked at him. “It might. That’s why I need to hear you.”

We talked for a long time. He did not turn his fear into an order. I did not
call it fearlessness that I wanted to leave. I was afraid of going and afraid
of staying because I was afraid. By the end we had no perfect agreement, only
the truth that he understood my choice and would feel its cost.

Luka asked practical questions. Zala was angry for a day and then called back
to ask more. Ana spoke with Matej about the journey they would have to make
together as a couple before he could make one with me. Nobody gave us a
blessing that removed the danger. Their love made the decision heavier and more
honest.

At the airport in late August, Eduard kissed me and kept his hand on my
shoulder a moment longer than usual. Ana hugged me. Matej and Eduard embraced
too. We were four people with a history that could not be arranged neatly for a
farewell photograph. For once I did not try.

“Don’t you know by now that I’m indestructible?” I said jokingly.

Eduard just smiled back.

Matej and I went towards the gate. We still did not know whether every part of
our route would open to us. People helping us had warned us how uncertain the
final crossing would be. I carried a small bag and a notebook with more
questions than plans. On the first blank page I wrote the words that had come
to me in the hospital: *Now – it’s the time.* I meant time to live the days I
had, to listen to the people whose lives our work would enter, and to remember
that none of us could decide how long those days would be.
