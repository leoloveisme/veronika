# Spain, here I come

Madrid, May 2004.

At the airport I did what I always did after leaving home: I called Eduard. He told me Luka had asked whether Spain was farther away than India. Zala had wanted to know why she could not come if there were oranges there.

“Tell her I'll bring some,” I said.

“She says she wants to choose them herself.”

“Then tell her I'll bring a story about the place where I chose them.”

“That might work.”

I heard a small voice demand the telephone, then Zala said, “Mama, you have to come back.”

“I will.”

The crew was waiting for me outside. I said goodbye before she could ask when.

Our route began in Madrid and ran towards Zaragoza, Barcelona, and Valencia before turning south. We had a schedule dense with interviews and filming locations. In my notebook I wrote the names of the cities in a column, as if putting them on paper would make me remember more than hotel rooms and the inside of a vehicle.

For the first few days, Madrid disappointed me. I had arrived expecting an instant change in myself. Instead I was tired, checking my notes, and trying to remember the questions I needed to ask the next morning. A city could not give me a revelation between breakfast and a recording deadline. Once I stopped asking it to, I noticed other things: the woman who corrected my pronunciation and then told me about her work, the waiter who remembered which of us wanted coffee, the long evening light that made it difficult to believe the day was finished.

I knew some Spanish words and used them badly. People answered in English when they heard me struggle. I asked them to let me try again. Each conversation took longer, and I remembered more of it. I bought a small notebook for expressions I heard and wanted to use. By the time we left Madrid, it was full of crossings-out.

The work gave me reasons to speak to people I would otherwise have passed. In Zaragoza I spent an afternoon with a family preparing for a celebration while we tried to keep cameras and cables out of their way. In Barcelona a woman we were interviewing stopped to ask about my children. I showed her their photograph. For a few minutes the crew waited while we compared the parts of parenting that had defeated us both. That exchange stayed with me longer than the polished answer she gave when the camera was on.

Every few nights I called home. Luka wanted reports on trains, boats, and anything with wheels. Zala asked whether I had found her oranges. Eduard told me what the children had done, then, after they went away, what he had painted. He was quiet with most people, but in these calls he could talk for half an hour about a colour he had mixed and then ask if I had eaten. I missed his voice most keenly after we hung up.

On the first of May, I watched news of Slovenia joining the European Union on a television in my hotel room. I sat on the edge of the bed until the report ended, although I knew what it would say. I thought of the documents I had gathered for India, of Eduard and me leaving Ljubljana, and of how often we had moved since. Membership would not erase the practical work of travelling or living elsewhere. It still mattered to me that the country we had grown up in was being welcomed into this larger arrangement. I called my mother and listened to her describe what people at home were saying.

When I spoke to Eduard later, he asked whether I felt different.

“Not yet,” I said. “Ask me when I get back.”

We went on through Valencia and down towards Andalusia. There were stretches when all I saw were the road ahead and the next place on our list. The camera could turn a morning into a beautiful few seconds; I knew what had been left outside the frame. I liked the work and the people I met through it. I also began to ask myself how much of a place I had experienced when I was always preparing to leave it.

In Granada, we finished late and ate together without discussing the next day's schedule. One of the crew asked what I would do when filming ended. I had kept several days free before my flight home. My first answer was that I would sleep. By morning I wanted something else.

We filmed farther south, passing through Marbella and Gibraltar, then returned by way of Seville and Córdoba. I had a photograph for Luka of each place where he would have liked to climb something. For Zala I kept postponing the oranges; fresh fruit was a poor choice for the bottom of a suitcase, and I wanted to bring her one she could hold rather than a picture of one. She would have considered that a fair distinction.

When the crew headed back towards Madrid, I stayed in Andalusia. I had heard about a small retreat in the hills and decided to visit for a day. The road climbed past olive groves towards a white village. By the time I arrived, I was prepared to be underwhelmed. I had taken a yoga session with other mothers in India. It had been pleasant, but I had spent most of it watching Luka crawl towards a door. I did not expect a mat on a floor to change the way I lived.

That morning we moved slowly. I was impatient with the instructor's request to notice where I was holding tension. I could have told her without closing my eyes: in my shoulders, my jaw, and somewhere behind my forehead, where the next thing I needed to do always seemed to be waiting.

Then she asked us to sit and pay attention to our breathing. I wanted an exercise with a result I could point to. Instead I heard air coming in and going out. I heard someone shift beside me. A bird called outside. My mind supplied the unfinished work, the children, the flight home, and an idea for a sentence I did not want to forget. I brought my attention back, lost it, and brought it back again.

No revelation arrived. When we finished, I felt how hard I had worked simply to stay in one place. I asked the instructor why something so ordinary required so much practice.

“Perhaps that's why we practise,” she said.

I stayed another day. I learned a breathing exercise I could repeat without anyone guiding me, and wrote the steps in my notebook beside my Spanish phrases. I made no grand promise to become a different woman. I wanted to know whether I could carry even a few minutes of that attention into a crowded airport or a sleepless night at home.

On my last evening I called Eduard from outside, where the air had cooled. He said Luka had helped him put away brushes and Zala had refused to let anyone else choose her bedtime story.

“I found something here,” I said.

“What?”

“I'm not sure yet. A way of slowing down, perhaps.”

There was a pause. Then he said, “I'd like to see that.”

I laughed because I knew what he meant. He had watched me plan the next journey before finishing the last one. I loved that part of myself and was tired of it too.

The next day I returned to Madrid for my flight. I bought oranges as late as I could and carried them in a separate bag. At home, Zala inspected each one before choosing the smallest. Luka wanted to see the photographs. Eduard took my suitcase, then stood in the doorway while the children talked over one another.

I had stories for all three of them. I also had a page of breathing instructions tucked among my notes. I would need the page again before the week was over.
