# WHAT IS IT ALL FOR?

For years I had believed I needed to go farther to see more clearly. After Iceland, the United States, and Latin America, I came home wanting to know what would happen if we stayed somewhere long enough to notice it change.

In 2011, Eduard and I moved within Britain to a place near the sea. We wanted more room for his work, ground where I could plant, and a door the children could leave through without stepping straight into city traffic. The new house needed repairs. The garden looked as though it had been waiting for someone with either patience or money. We had both, at least in uneven amounts.

On our first morning there I walked to the water before breakfast. It was not the ocean in Iceland. There were houses behind me and other people out with dogs. Still, the horizon was open, and the air carried salt into the streets. I thought that if I could come here whenever I wanted, I might finally stop chasing the next place.

For a while, I did.

Eduard made a studio and filled it more slowly than I filled the garden. Luka found a corner where nobody would disturb the things he was drawing. Zala explored every room, decided which one should have been hers, and tried to negotiate an exchange. We ate dinner among unpacked boxes. We learned where the wind reached the house, where the light fell in the afternoon, and which plants would survive my absences.

I still travelled for work. The programmes brought me to places I would never have seen otherwise and into conversations I was grateful to have. Some books sold well enough that I stopped pretending every new contract was an accident. Eduard's paintings went to galleries in cities we could once only have afforded to visit briefly. When we needed help with the children or the house, we could pay for it. I knew what an advantage that was. It did not make the hours longer.

The children grew without asking whether I was ready. Luka no longer wanted me to read every page he wrote or look at a drawing before he considered it finished. If I waited, he usually brought it to me himself. Zala wanted privacy and an audience, sometimes in the same afternoon. She argued with me, borrowed my clothes, and asked questions whose answers she did not always want. I liked the people they were becoming. I missed the children who had once fit against me on the sofa. Those feelings could coexist without either child owing me a return to the past.

One autumn evening I found Eduard in the kitchen with a calendar open between him and Luka. Our son had arranged to spend a weekend away with friends. Eduard was asking about travel and times; Luka answered patiently, with the expression of someone who had already answered twice. I nearly added my own questions. Instead I went outside and checked the plants, though there was nothing they needed from me.

When I came back in, Luka had gone upstairs.

“You knew about the trip?” I asked.

“He told us last week.”

“He told you.”

“He told us at dinner. You were reading a message from work.”

I remembered the evening and not the conversation. That hurt more than if he had deliberately left me out.

I put the message away the next night. There were other evenings when I forgot. I could hear the difference between saying my family mattered most and arranging my attention as though that were true. Work was not an intruder in our life; it was part of what I loved and part of how I knew myself. I did not want to blame it for every moment I missed. I wanted to notice when I was choosing it.

As the years passed, I went into the sea in every season. Sometimes I swam with others. Sometimes I walked the shore and decided the water could wait. The cold still brought me sharply into my body, but it no longer gave me a new story each time. I could not go on treating every swim as proof that I had chosen life. I had made that choice long ago. The question now was how I used the ordinary days between the memorable ones.

On days at home I wrote, gardened, and occasionally sat beside Eduard while he painted. We could spend an hour in comfortable silence. There were also evenings when we seemed to speak only to keep the house running.

“Can you collect this?”

“Do we need anything from the shop?”

“What would you like to eat?”

We answered each other kindly. Afterwards I would wonder when we had last talked without solving a small practical problem. I had once thought that a full life would protect us from that sort of quiet. I was learning that love required attention even when nothing dramatic had gone wrong.

I asked Eduard to walk with me one evening. He put down his brush and came. At first we spoke about Luka and Zala. Then he told me he had been working on a painting for months and still could not see its centre. I said I knew the feeling. He stopped and looked at me.

“Is this about your book?”

“Perhaps. It might be about everything.”

I tried to explain. We had the home, the work, the children, enough money, a garden full of things I had planted. When I was younger I would have named those gifts as an answer. Now the list left me grateful and still asking.

“What is it all for?” I said.

Eduard watched the path ahead of us. “Do you need one answer?”

“I'd like one.”

“I'm not sure I have it.”

I was relieved that he did not offer a beautiful sentence to close the subject. We walked until it grew dark. At home we made dinner together, and for once neither of us reached for a telephone while we ate.

Luka left home first. His absence was quieter than I expected. A chair stood empty, and I kept setting a place for him because I had done it for so many years. Zala stayed a while longer. When she left, she filled the doorway with bags and instructions about what not to do in her room. Afterwards the house seemed larger and less useful. I had wanted the children to become free enough to leave us. I was proud of them. For weeks I walked past their rooms and had to remind myself that freedom felt different from this side of the door.

Eduard and I were parents all the time, but no longer full-time parents in the old sense. We had more hours together and, at first, fewer things to say in them. He painted. I wrote and took assignments. We visited the children when they wanted us and learned to wait for their invitations when they did not. Some days we enjoyed the space they had left. Some days I wanted the old noise back, including the arguments I had once wished would end.

Near the end of the decade I sat outside after a swim with my notebook open. I had written the question in capital letters at the top of a page: *WHAT IS IT ALL FOR?* Beneath it were several beginnings, all crossed out. I closed the book when I heard Eduard coming down the path.

“Any answer?” he asked.

“Not yet.”

He sat beside me. We watched the sea and then went home because we were cold and hungry. I did not take the page as evidence that something was wrong with our life. I took it as evidence that the question was still alive inside a life I valued. I wanted to keep asking it without fleeing every time I could not answer.

By 2020 the question would meet a world that had begun to close its doors. For the moment ours was open. I went inside with Eduard and put the kettle on.
