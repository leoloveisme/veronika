# Madness or bravery

Iceland, July 2010.

I had travelled with swimmers who knew how to turn a winter morning into a shared occasion. In Poland, Wim Hof had guided us up and down a mountain in swimwear before we entered cold water together. Later I had filmed with people whose company made the first step easier. Someone always had a joke or a towel ready. Even when the water demanded my full attention, I knew where the others were.

In Iceland I wanted to find out what would happen without them.

I had come partly for work and stayed a few days after the crew left. The country was not the bare, white place I had pictured from other people's photographs. There were roads, shops, people going home at the end of a working day. Yet there were also stretches of coast where the road seemed to end against something immeasurably larger. I drove through one of them with my swimming things beside me and stopped when I saw the ocean.

The North Atlantic stretched beneath a pale sky with no opposite shore to hold my eye. Waves broke against the dark beach and withdrew, each one leaving the stones to settle with a sound like a thousand small bones. I walked the shoreline and watched for a long time. I knew the water would be cold. I could not know what the sea would do next. I was here alone. There was no group to decide the moment for me, no camera to turn hesitation into a scene. If I chose not to swim, no one would know.

That thought made me angry. I had told Eduard that I wanted a few days without a schedule. I had told myself I wanted solitude. Now I was using it to ask whether my courage existed only when someone else could see it.

I sat on a stone and called him. He answered from the studio. I could hear the children somewhere behind him.

“Are you coming home tomorrow?” he asked.

“That's the plan.”

“Have you found what you went looking for?”

“I found the ocean.”

“That wasn't what I meant.”

“I know.”

He waited. I could have told him what I was considering and asked him to talk me out of it. I could also have kept quiet and let him think I was calling only because I missed his voice. I chose the harder conversation.

“I want to swim here,” I said. “Alone.”

“Why alone?”

“I've done it with other people. I want to know what it's like when there is no one to follow.”

“Is that a reason you trust?”

I looked at the water. “I'm trying to find out.”

He did not give me permission. He did not pretend to forbid me. We spoke for a while about the children and the journey home, then said goodbye. When the call ended, I was no less alone, but I had stopped hiding the choice from the person it would frighten most.

I walked the shore again. Wind roughened the surface beyond the breaking waves. A part of me kept offering magnificent explanations: I was mastering fear; I was proving that the girl who had nearly drowned could enter any water she chose. Another part asked a smaller question: did I actually want to swim, or did I want to be able to say that I had?

I thought of the old prediction that I would die by drowning. For years I had called it nonsense and still heard it whenever water rose above my chest. My childhood fear did not care what I believed. It arrived now as a memory of losing the sky. I stood facing the ocean and waited for the memory to loosen its hold.

“Madness or bravery?” I said aloud. There was no one there to answer.

I could leave. Nothing in my work, my marriage, or my life required this swim. I could also enter, knowing that deciding to do something did not make it wise. I had used the phrase *nothing is under control* when a plan failed and I had to improvise. At the edge of the ocean, it sounded less like a motto than a fact. My choice was the part I could own; the sea would remain itself.

I changed and went in. Cold closed around my legs. A wave struck harder than the one before it and made me step back. I waited, watching the next swell rise and fall, then went forward again. The shore was behind me; ahead there was only water and sky. I began to swim.

Without voices around me, the sound of each stroke seemed louder. The ocean lifted me, let me fall, and lifted me again. I looked back. The beach appeared smaller than I expected, a dark edge beneath the cliffs. I turned towards it before my wish to go farther became an argument I would have to win.

For a moment on the way in, my fear returned sharply. A wave hid the beach from me and I remembered the childhood instant when I had lost the sky. I wanted land at once. I could not make the distance disappear by wanting it. I kept moving until the dark stones came back into view, then closer, until my feet found the ground.

I climbed out and stood bent forward, breathing. There was no applause and no camera. My hands trembled as I reached for the towel. I was relieved to be out, and I was glad I had gone in. Those feelings could sit beside each other without either one proving I had been right.

Back in dry clothes, I remained on the shore until I was ready to walk to the car. My swim had left no visible mark. Another wave broke where I had entered, indifferent to whether I was still there. I had not conquered the ocean. I had gone into it and returned.

That evening I called Eduard again. He let the children finish telling me about their day before asking about mine.

“I swam,” I said.

“Are you all right?”

“Yes.”

“And what did you find?”

I wanted a beautiful answer for him. What I had was less impressive.

“I was afraid,” I said. “I went in. I came back.”

“I can live with the last part.”

“So can I.”

Later, alone in my room, I wondered whether the swim had been brave or foolish. I still could not sort it cleanly. I had wanted to meet my fear without making a performance of it; I had also taken a risk no one had asked me to take. I would remember the ocean's size and Eduard's question about whether I trusted my reason. Both belonged to the day.

I was due to leave for the United States next. My schedule was crowded again. Before sleeping, I put my notebook on the bedside table and wrote only a few words: *The water did not need me to be fearless.*
