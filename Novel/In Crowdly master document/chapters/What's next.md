# What's next?

By January 1999, the question had stopped sounding like a joke.

Eduard had asked it on the morning we left Villete behind: what shall we do
next? We had answered with a wedding. Now we were leaving Ljubljana for Bern
with two suitcases, a box of paints, and a child who made her presence known by
kicking me under the ribs whenever I tried to sleep.

At the station, my mother pressed food into my hands as if Switzerland had
none. His father embraced him, then stood back to look at the box of paintings
we had wrapped for the journey.

“Send us a photograph of the house,” his mother said.

“We haven't seen it ourselves,” I told her.

“Then send two photographs.”

We laughed. I kept the food. I was hungry again before we crossed the border.

The house was small and stood beside a quiet road. Behind it was an even
smaller building that looked towards the Aare. Eduard went into that one first.
He stood in its empty room, studying the light on the walls, while I stood in
the doorway with a hand against my belly.

“You've claimed it,” I said.

“I haven't said anything.”

“You don't need to.”

He smiled and carried the box of paints inside. By evening there were brushes
in jars, canvases against the wall, and a chair he had positioned to face the
river. Our belongings still filled the main house in uneven piles. I didn't
mind. Seeing him at work made the move feel real.

His paintings had begun to sell before we left Slovenia. In Bern, more people
wanted to see them. His parents knew people in the art world and had helped
introduce his work, though Eduard disliked it when I suggested that their
introductions explained everything. They had opened a door, he said. The
paintings had to stand on their own once they were inside.

Money came in faster than either of us had expected. I enjoyed the absurdity of
it. We could buy a table without first calculating which other thing we would
have to do without. Eduard looked at the figures, put some aside, and looked at
them again.

“You're allowed to be pleased,” I said one evening.

“I am pleased.”

“You look as though someone has presented you with a bill.”

“Perhaps they will.”

He was joking, mostly. He knew how quickly a gallery's enthusiasm could change.
I loved the paintings and wanted to believe everyone else would go on loving
them forever. He kept painting, kept saving, and let me be the one to celebrate
aloud when a canvas sold.

On 10 February, Luka was born.

There was nothing quiet about the hours before he arrived. Afterwards the room
settled around a sound so small I almost missed it: his breath. Eduard sat
beside us and watched our son as closely as he had watched the river on our
first day in the house. When I passed Luka to him, he held him with both hands
and asked, very seriously, whether he was doing it correctly.

“I've known him for only a few minutes longer than you have,” I said.

Luka slept. He woke to feed and went back to sleep. The nurses called us lucky,
and we were. I was also sore, tired, and frightened by how completely this
little stranger depended on me. People spoke to me as if giving birth had
delivered a set of instructions into my head along with the baby. When I didn't
know why Luka was crying, I felt embarrassed to ask. When he stopped, I
wondered if I had understood him or if he had simply exhausted himself.

At home Eduard learned the same things I did, one uncertain hour at a time. He
changed Luka while I tried to sleep. I fed Luka while Eduard slept. Some
mornings we managed both. I had expected love to make motherhood easy. Love was
there, unmistakably, even when I wanted to put Luka down and have ten minutes
in which no one needed my body. I could adore him and still want those ten
minutes. It took me longer than it should have to admit that.

Once, while Luka slept against my chest, I thought of the woman I had been in
Villete. She had imagined an end to her life, but she had never imagined this
particular weight, warm and breathing beneath her chin. I did not know whether
she would have believed me if I had told her about him. I wished, for a moment,
that she could have met him.

I began to write in the hours Luka gave me. At first I filled the backs of
envelopes and the margins of our household lists. I wrote down things I wanted
to remember: the way Eduard spoke to our son when he thought I was asleep, the
shape the river made beyond his studio window, the bewildering advice strangers
gave a new mother. Some days words came faster than I could put them on paper.
Other days I carried a sentence in my head until evening and discovered it was
all I had.

Eduard read a page when I finally let him. He came from the studio with a dark
stripe of paint across one wrist and sat beside me at the kitchen table. I
watched his face instead of the paper.

“Well?” I asked.

“You've made me sound very solemn.”

“You are solemn.”

“Only when you're watching.”

He pointed to a line I had written about Luka. “Keep this,” he said.

It pleased me more than I wanted to show. I had loved speaking and telling
stories long before I had anything resembling a career. Now there was a small
person asleep in the next room, a husband painting by the river, and a stack of
pages on the table. I wanted to see what I could make of them.

One afternoon I took Luka out while Eduard worked. At the river I stopped to
adjust the blanket around him. The water moved past us, indifferent to whether
I knew how to be a mother or a writer, whether Eduard's next painting sold,
whether we stayed in Bern for one year or twenty. Luka opened his eyes, looked
up at me, and closed them again.

I had spent so much time asking what came next. For once, I could wait for the
answer. We went home before he grew hungry.
