# Preparing for India

The first thing I packed for India was a folder.

It lay open on our kitchen table in Bern, swallowing photographs, forms, our passports, and a list I kept rewriting because crossing out an item did not stop me worrying about it. I wanted to travel for months, not for a hurried holiday. Luka would be coming with me. At nine months old he had no opinion about India that he could express, but he had firm opinions about when he wanted to eat, and all of them required me.

Eduard came in from the studio with paint on the heel of his hand. He picked up the list.

“You've written passports twice.”

“One for each of us.”

“You've written mine as well.”

“In case you decide to come.”

He placed the paper back on the table. “You want me to?”

I did, and I didn't. I wanted him beside me on the journey. I also wanted to find out what it was like to go somewhere neither of us had been and make my own way through it. Since Luka's birth, almost every decision had begun with what the three of us needed. I loved our three, but there was still an *I* inside it, asking to be heard.

“Stay and paint,” I told him. “Come later, if you can.”

He looked towards Luka, who was asleep in the next room. “Call me when you get there.”

“I will.”

“And when you go somewhere else.”

“I won't spend the whole trip beside a telephone.”

“I know.” He kissed the top of my head. “Call anyway.”

The trip to Vienna was a rehearsal for the larger one. I left Bern with Luka, the folder, a bag of his clothes, a bag of mine, and the ridiculous belief that I could keep them all within reach. By the time we arrived, a clean shirt had gone missing, one of the papers had slipped from its place, and Luka had fallen asleep just as I needed him awake.

At the Indian consulate I waited with him against my shoulder. When our turn came, the clerk examined the application and looked up at me.

“Why do you want a visa for six months?”

“Because I would like to see your country.”

“That takes six months?”

“I suspect it takes longer.”

He looked at Luka. I tightened my hold before I could help myself.

“You are travelling with the baby?”

“Yes.”

“For the whole time?”

“That is the plan.”

I could hear how determined I sounded. The truth was less tidy. I had routes drawn across a map and places I wanted to visit, but I did not know how far we would get before one of us became ill or tired or wanted to go home. I did not offer the clerk any of that. I handed him the next document he requested and waited while he checked it.

Outside, Vienna went on as though the fate of my trip were a matter of no importance. I walked until Luka woke, then found a café and fed him while my own lunch grew cold. A woman at the next table smiled at him and asked where we were going. When I told her, she looked from his small face to mine.

“All the way to India?”

“All the way,” I said.

I was beginning to hear the distance in those words myself.

We spent the night in a hotel. I laid out the clothes for the morning and checked the folder once more. Luka reached for a loose page, caught its corner, and laughed when I took it away. There was no use explaining passports to him. We lay down together, and he fell asleep nursing while I was still making lists in my head. At some point I slept too.

The next morning he woke before I did and studied my face as if he had been waiting patiently for me to return. We went down to breakfast. Two people at a nearby table asked his name, and for a few minutes we talked about children and trains instead of visas. I felt almost rested when I went back to the consulate.

I collected the passports and walked out with them in my hand. On the pavement I stopped, opened mine, and read the name, the dates, and then the name again. I checked Luka's as carefully. Everything was in order.

I had expected to feel triumphant. Instead I felt the first small weight of what I had arranged. The papers would let us enter India; they would not tell me what to do when we arrived. I put them away before I could read them a third time and took Luka for a walk.

Back in Bern, departure acquired a date. I washed the clothes we would need, packed and unpacked them, and tried to imagine which things would matter once we were far from home. Eduard took Luka whenever he could. His paintings had begun to sell, and I could see how much work was waiting in the studio, but he never made me feel that our son was an interruption to it.

The evening before we left, I put Luka to bed and switched on the baby monitor. For once the suitcase was closed. Eduard was still working across the yard. I took a thick wool blanket from the bedroom and went to find him.

He turned when I entered, a brush in his hand.

“Did you forget something?”

“You.”

I spread the blanket on a clear patch of floor. He set the brush down, looking amused and a little uncertain, and came when I held out my hand. All day we had spoken about tickets and bags and the hour we needed to leave. Now there was nothing useful to arrange. I kissed him. He kissed me back, and we stayed there together until Luka's voice reached us through the monitor.

In the morning Eduard drove us to the airport. He lifted the bags out of the car, then held Luka while I searched for the passports I had checked a dozen times. They were exactly where I had put them.

At the gate I kissed Eduard and took our son from him. He smoothed Luka's hair, then touched my cheek.

“Call,” he said.

“When we arrive.”

I went through the gate and turned once. Eduard was still there. I raised my hand, and he raised his. Then Luka twisted in my arms to look at the people ahead of us, and I carried him towards the plane.
