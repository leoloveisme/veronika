# One, two, three - swimming we go

After Thailand I kept ending my showers with cold water. On some mornings I stepped out almost at once. On others I stayed until I could breathe without arguing with the tap. I had begun to wonder what people who spent whole winters in cold water knew that I didn't.

I cannot tell you exactly how I heard about Wim Hof. Someone may have sent me a short film, or I may have found an article while looking for swimmers to interview. What I remember is a notice for a guided course in Poland and the sudden certainty that I wanted to go. I had been turning the idea of an ice-swimming documentary over in my head; here was a place to start learning before I asked anyone to put a camera on me.

I showed Eduard the course details. He read them twice.

“Poland? In winter?”

“That's when the water will be cold.”

“I understood that part.”

“I want to know what I'm doing before I take this idea to a producer.”

He looked at me over the page. “And if you decide it isn't for you?”

“Then I'll know that too.”

I went to Poland at the beginning of 2008. I took my notebook and a small camera for research, with permission to film parts of the course. The other participants had come for different reasons. Some had experience in cold water; some, like me, had questions they could not answer from the bank. We were there to learn with Wim Hof guiding the group. I was relieved to be a participant before I was a presenter.

The first morning I listened so hard that I almost forgot to write. We talked about attention, about recognising when confidence ran ahead of judgment, and about keeping one another in sight. Wim led the session. I was most interested in the moments when someone admitted uncertainty and another person made room for it. I recognised the fear I had carried since the childhood day I nearly drowned. I could bring it with me; I did not have to pretend it had vanished.

People noticed my appearance before they knew why I was there. I had lived with that long enough to recognise both the advantage and the irritation. One participant asked whether I planned to take part or only present a segment. I told him he would have his answer when we went outside.

In temperatures below zero, we set out to walk up a mountain and back down in our swimwear. I had seen photographs of people doing this and assumed the camera concealed a warmer reality just outside the frame. There was no such trick for my bare arms. Snow lay beside the path, and the cold found every place my swimsuit did not cover. Wim guided the group uphill. I kept close enough to see the people ahead of me.

At first I wanted to turn the climb into a contest with myself. I watched one participant moving easily and tried to match her pace. Then I lost the rhythm of my steps and stopped. The group waited. I looked down at the ground I had covered and up at the stretch that remained. I could continue, but I did not have to prove it by pretending I was comfortable. I climbed on with the others, aware of how readily pride had made me stop paying attention.

At the top we paused together. I thought reaching it would settle the argument in my head; instead I looked at the way down and realised I was only halfway through. The descent demanded its own attention. My legs were tired, the path was uneven, and I could not hurry simply because I wanted to be warm. I watched where I put my feet and stayed with the group until we came off the mountain.

Then we went for the cold-water dip. I had expected to want nothing more to do with the cold after the walk. At the water's edge, the memory of my childhood lake returned: the lost footing, the sky I could not find. I heard the other participants beside me and looked at the water in front of me. I knew where the group would enter and where we would come out.

I went in under the course's guidance. The cold reached me all at once. I stayed with the group, moved a short distance, and returned to the bank. Someone passed me a towel. I was shaking, laughing, and far less certain of myself than I had been when I booked the trip. The mountain had demanded that I keep going up and then down. The water asked me to choose to enter after I had already done both.

Back inside, I wrapped myself in dry clothes and held a warm cup between my hands. What I remembered most clearly was not how long we had walked or stayed in the water. It was the moment I stopped comparing myself with the person ahead and noticed where I stood.

That evening I watched the few minutes we had filmed. The camera had caught me hesitating. I kept the shot. I also filmed conversations when people wanted to speak and put the camera away when they did not. I had arrived wanting to find an image strong enough to begin a documentary. What stayed with me instead was the group: their very different reasons for entering, the attention they paid to each other, and the ordinary relief of getting warm again.

At home the children wanted to know whether the water had been colder than the shower. Luka asked how long I had stayed in. Zala wanted to see the footage and complained that the camera had spent too much time on faces when she wanted to see what was below the surface. Eduard watched quietly, then asked whether I still remembered the lake from my childhood when I swam.

“Before I get in,” I said. “Once I'm moving, I have to pay attention to where I am.”

He nodded. He did not tell me the fear had been cured. I was glad. It hadn't.

I took the footage to a producer. She liked the Polish course but asked what would happen after it. I wanted to meet people who swam regularly, not only people who could take a week away to learn. A course could introduce me to the cold; a continuing practice would show what I did with that introduction. We agreed to film as I found other swimmers and, at times, went into the water with them.

I first visited a local group without the crew. From the bank I heard their complaints, jokes, and arguments about whose turn it was to enter. They had work and families waiting after the swim. The sight of them returning, drying off, and asking one another about breakfast interested me as much as the water itself.

When I joined them, I told a woman about the childhood accident that still came to mind whenever I lost sight of the bottom. She showed me where she entered and where she came out. Another swimmer stayed nearby. We went in together. I kept my head above the surface and swam only as far as I had agreed to go. My confidence did not grow in a straight line: a difficult morning could follow an easy one. I began to trust the practice because there was room in it for both.

The crew returned with me. I asked them to keep filming when I hesitated. I wanted the documentary to include the people who had taught me to continue and to show the choices surrounding a swim, not only a dramatic plunge. A woman who swam to mark the end of her working week, a man who brought tea for everyone but rarely got in, and the course participants in Poland all belonged in the same film. Each showed me a different way to meet the water.

As we gathered more material, I met swimmers and divers whose relationship with water was very different from mine. I spent time filming with Stig Severinsen and talking about what a camera might capture of breath, discipline, and the moments before entering the water. I did not want every person in the programme to speak for the same idea. Their differences made the film better.

The project kept expanding after those first seasons. Later I swam and filmed with Johanna Nordblad and Elina Mäkinen as well. I wanted women in the documentary whose presence could not be reduced to my own face on a poster. Each had a way of moving through cold water that made me reconsider what I thought I knew. We gave them space to describe their work in their own words, then went swimming together.

Editing was harder than entering the water. A shot of me gasping on the bank could be made to look reckless or triumphant depending on what came before it. A quiet conversation with a swimmer could disappear because someone thought viewers needed another dramatic plunge. I argued for the ordinary moments: towels passed from hand to hand, a person choosing not to swim that day, laughter after everyone had come out. The film was about the decision to enter and the decision to return, not only the seconds in between.

When the documentary reached viewers, some wrote to say it had encouraged them to face an old fear. Others called it crazy, or said showing such swims would persuade people to mistake a dramatic image for good judgment. I understood part of the criticism. Television had taught me how readily a difficult experience could be cut into a dare. I hoped the full film showed the people, preparation, and limits around the swims. I could not control which part any viewer would remember.

One evening Eduard found me reading the responses again.

“You've read that one already,” he said.

“I know.”

“Does reading it twice improve it?”

“No.”

He sat down beside me. On the screen a stranger had written that I was courting death. I wanted to answer with the story of Villete, the years since, and every morning I had chosen to come home from the water. I closed the page instead. The stranger had seen a few minutes of a life I was still learning to understand myself.

Years before the finished film reached viewers, I was already looking at photographs of Iceland. Swimming had given me neither a cure for fear nor an answer to the question I had taken to Thailand. It had given me people I trusted, a practice I could return to, and a more exact sense of where my choices ended and the water began. I wanted to know what I would feel without the group beside me to count the strokes. In 2010, that question became the beginning of another journey.
