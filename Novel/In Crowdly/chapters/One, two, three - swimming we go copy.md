# One, two, three - swimming we go

I cannot tell you when I first decided to make a documentary about ice swimming. Perhaps I saw a short film, or heard someone describe the feeling of stepping into winter water. The idea had no single moment of arrival. By the time I noticed it, I had begun collecting names and asking the production team how far we could take a camera in the cold.

After Thailand I had kept ending my showers with cold water. On some days I turned the tap back almost at once. On others I stayed until the first urge to run had passed. It was an odd habit to explain to anyone else, and for a while I did not need to. It was something I could do before the children woke, while the rest of the house still belonged to sleep.

I also found people who swam outdoors through the colder months. I went first to watch. From the bank I could hear their voices carrying over the water: complaints, jokes, arguments about whose turn it was to get in. They were ordinary people with work to do afterwards. The sight of them returning, drying off, and asking one another about breakfast interested me as much as the swim itself.

When I joined them, I told the group about the childhood accident that still came to mind whenever I lost sight of the bottom. Nobody tried to talk me out of remembering it. One woman showed me where she entered and where she came out. Another said she would stay near me. I liked the absence of speeches. We could take the water seriously without pretending it would make us extraordinary.

We went in together. I kept my head above the surface and swam only as far as I had agreed to go. The cold pressed against my face. For a few strokes I heard nothing but my breathing and the movement of water near my ears. Then I reached the place where we were turning back. On shore, someone handed me my towel before I had managed to ask for it.

“Again next week?” the woman beside me said.

“Ask me next week.”

I came back. Sometimes I wanted to be there; sometimes I dreaded the first step all the way from home. I learned the names of the other swimmers, their habits, and the small courtesies that kept a group together. My confidence did not grow in a straight line. A difficult morning could follow an easy one. I began to trust the practice because there was room in it for both.

A producer saw my notes and asked what the programme would be about. I had written down more than temperatures and distances. I had written about a woman who used her swim to mark the end of a working week, a man who brought tea for everyone but rarely entered the water himself, and the different reasons people had for returning. I wanted to film those reasons as closely as the water.

“We'll need you in it,” the producer said.

“I know.”

“You don't sound pleased.”

“I don't want a film where everyone else exists to prove I'm brave.”

We agreed to begin with the group and see what the footage told us. I was used to standing before a camera to ask questions. I was less comfortable watching myself approach the water. On the first filming day, I asked the crew to keep rolling when I hesitated. If that was part of the experience, I wanted it in the film.

The project grew beyond the people who had taught me to begin. In January 2008 I joined a course with Wim Hof. Some participants had come for the cold; others were interested in breath and attention. We filmed when we had permission and put the cameras aside when a person wanted to practise without them. I listened, asked questions, and swam with the group. I was aware of the lens whenever I approached the water. I was also aware that a lens could not do the swimming for me.

People noticed my appearance before they knew why I was there. I had lived with that long enough to recognise both the advantage and the irritation. Someone asked whether I planned to get in or only present the segment. I told him he would have his answer when the others got in. Afterwards I asked him why he had assumed those were different jobs.

At home the children wanted to know whether the water had been colder than the shower. Luka asked how long I had stayed in. Zala wanted to see the footage and complained that the camera had spent too much time on faces when she wanted to see what was below the surface. Eduard watched quietly, then asked whether I still remembered the lake from my childhood when I swam.

“Sometimes,” I said. “I remember it before I get in. Once I'm moving, I have to pay attention to where I am.”

He nodded. He did not tell me the fear had been cured. I was glad. It hadn't.

As we gathered more material, I met swimmers and divers whose relationship with water was very different from mine. I spent time filming with Stig Severinsen and talking about what a camera might capture of breath, discipline, and the moments before entering the water. I did not want every person in the programme to speak for the same idea. Their differences made the film better.

The project kept expanding after those first seasons. Later I swam and filmed with Johanna Nordblad and Elina Mäkinen as well. I wanted women in the documentary whose presence could not be reduced to my own face on a poster. Each had a way of moving through cold water that made me reconsider what I thought I knew. We gave them space to describe their work in their own words, then went swimming together.

Editing was harder than entering the water. A shot of me gasping on the bank could be made to look reckless or triumphant depending on what came before it. A quiet conversation with a swimmer could disappear because someone thought viewers needed another dramatic plunge. I argued for the ordinary moments: towels passed from hand to hand, a person choosing not to swim that day, laughter after everyone had come out. The film was about the decision to enter and the decision to return, not only the seconds in between.

When the documentary reached viewers, some wrote to say it had encouraged them to face an old fear. Others called it crazy, or said showing such swims would persuade people to mistake a dramatic image for good judgment. I understood part of the criticism. Television had taught me how readily a difficult experience could be cut into a dare. I hoped the full film showed the people, preparation, and limits around the swims. I could not control which part any viewer would remember.

One evening Eduard found me reading the responses again.

“You've read that one already,” he said.

“I know.”

“Does reading it twice improve it?”

“No.”

He sat down beside me. On the screen a stranger had written that I was courting death. I wanted to answer with the story of Villete, the years since, and every morning I had chosen to come home from the water. I closed the page instead. The stranger had seen a few minutes of a life I was still learning to understand myself.

Years before the finished film reached viewers, I was already looking at photographs of Iceland. Swimming had given me neither a cure for fear nor an answer to the question I had taken to Thailand. It had given me people I trusted, a practice I could return to, and a more exact sense of where my choices ended and the water began. I wanted to know what I would feel without the group beside me to count the strokes. In 2010, that question became the beginning of another journey.
