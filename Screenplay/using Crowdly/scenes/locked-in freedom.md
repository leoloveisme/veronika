# Locked-in freedom

The doors did close. At first I imagined we would all open them again in a few weeks, blinking at the light and laughing at the things we had done to pass the time. Then the weeks lost their edges. My calendar filled with crossed-out journeys. The house by the sea, which I had chosen because it gave us room to breathe, became the place where nearly everything happened.

Eduard painted. I wrote when I could bear another hour at my desk. We called the children more often than they wanted us to. Some mornings I walked down to the shore before either of us had spoken. I could still hear the water from the path. When I was able to enter it, the cold took my breath and returned it to me. The horizon looked as wide as it had the year before, though the rest of my world had narrowed.

I was lucky to have that horizon. I knew people shut inside small flats, people who had to keep working among strangers, people who were afraid for someone they loved. Our own life had space and money and a garden. None of that stopped me from becoming restless. I could feel myself turning a privilege into an argument: if I could stand at the edge of the sea, surely no one could tell me I was confined.

“You can be here and still be furious,” Eduard said one evening.

“I'm not furious.”

He looked at the table, where I had laid out three versions of a journey I could not make.

“All right,” he said. “Then you are planning very loudly.”

I laughed, though I was not ready to put the papers away. After years of telling myself I was free because I could leave, staying in one place felt like a test I had never agreed to take. I had thought the question in my notebook—*What is it all for?*—might lead me somewhere. Now I had time to sit with it, and mostly I wanted to go anywhere else.

Zala called from her room one afternoon with her laptop balanced against a stack of books. She had begun tutoring English online. I heard her voice through the door, patient and clear, explaining a phrase for the third time. When her lesson ended she emerged carrying a cup of cold tea and three messages she had not answered.

“How was it?” I asked.

“He understood it in the end. That's something.”

The work gave her a schedule and some money of her own. It did not give her the crowded rooms, dancing, friends and unexpected conversations she missed. She tried other ways of earning online, including a brief experiment with OnlyFans. She told me about it herself, daring me to make a face. I asked what she wanted from it. After a while she decided it was not for her and stopped. What stayed with me was how little of her life I could know unless she chose to tell me.

She had learned Spanish from friends, music, long conversations, and a young man she was seeing when the world began to close. At dinner she would glance at her phone and smile before she remembered we could see her. Once Eduard caught the words *mi amor* on the screen.

“And who is that?” he asked.

“Someone who doesn't ask questions over dinner.”

“Then he wouldn't enjoy this family,” Eduard said.

Zala leaned towards me and said in Spanish, “He's impossible.” She meant her father, but her smile belonged to someone else. Her boyfriend came to the house when visits were possible. He cooked with her, left his coat over a chair, and spoke so quickly that I sometimes lost the thread even as Zala answered without hesitation. I loved seeing her so at ease in another language. I also saw how much of herself she had made beyond us.

Luka called less often, but when he did, he stayed on the line. He had found someone through Bumble, a woman five years older than he was. At first he told us almost nothing. His earlier romances had passed through the orbit of Zala's friends; now he sounded surprised by the steadiness of this one. He spoke of ordinary things they did together, and he sounded happy. I wanted to ask her name, her family, her plans, all at once. I let him tell me what he wished.

The arguments began when vaccination became a choice we each had to face. Eduard read, made up his mind, and got vaccinated. Luka did too.

“I don't mind,” Luka told me when I asked. “It feels right for me.”

I did mind. I distrusted the pressure I felt around the decision, and I did not want it made for me. I had relied on my intuition through changes that once frightened me. I lived away from the crowded city now. I swam throughout the year and felt strong in my body. I told myself I was not afraid of death. When I said, “I'll die when it is my time,” Eduard put down his cup.

“I know you're not afraid,” he said. “I am allowed to be.”

I had been speaking as though courage settled the matter for both of us. It didn't. His decision was his, and mine was mine. I could ask him to respect that without treating his concern as weakness. Some evenings we managed to listen to each other. Other evenings we repeated the same sentences until one of us went to bed.

Zala was firmer than I was. She did not want to be vaccinated. Her boyfriend did, and he wanted her to follow him. At first they argued about information, then about what each of them thought the other was asking. She felt pressed. He felt shut out. They broke up after a conversation neither of them could make gentle.

I found her in the kitchen afterwards, turning her telephone face down and then turning it over again.

“You liked him,” I said.

“I still do.”

I sat beside her. I wanted to tell her she would meet someone else, that she had been right, that leaving him was proof of something. I could see she needed none of those speeches. She wanted to be sad about a person she had loved.

For a while the four of us seemed to live in different countries even when we were only a call away. Eduard and I shared a house and made different choices. Luka built a life with a woman I knew mostly through the change in his voice. Zala taught English through a screen while searching for a place she could move through on her own terms. I had spent years encouraging them to think for themselves. Their independence was easy to admire until it led them somewhere I had not chosen.

In the summer of 2021 Zala began to talk about Mexico. She researched the journey, saved what she could from tutoring, and found people who might help her settle when she arrived. I tried to ask practical questions without disguising my wish that she would stay.

“I can come back,” she said.

“I know.”

“You don't sound as if you know.”

On 6 August she left. At the airport she hugged Eduard, then me, and stepped away before any of us could turn the farewell into a negotiation. I watched her join the queue with a bag she had packed herself. The world had felt closed to me for so long that I almost forgot a door could open for someone else first.

At home, I looked at the routes I had drawn months earlier. Some were no longer possible; others had changed. I went back to the shore, swam, came home and told Eduard I wanted to visit her in September.

“Because you're worried?” he asked.

“Yes. And because I want to see where she's gone.”

“Those are different reasons.”

“I know.”

He helped me think through the journey. We had learned to disagree without always arriving at an answer together. I was grateful that he could still help me pack.

Mexico brought heat, street noise, unfamiliar streets to learn, and Zala waiting for me with a confidence I had not seen in her at home. She showed me where she bought fruit, introduced me to friends, and corrected my directions when I took a wrong turn. Some days we spent hours together. On others she went about her life, and I had to remember that visiting her did not make me its centre.

One evening we walked until our feet hurt. Zala was telling me about a student who had made her laugh, and I realised I had followed her out of our old house without bringing its arguments into every conversation. I still worried. I still had my views, and she had hers. But we could be two women on a street, hungry and looking for dinner. It was a small freedom, and I had nearly missed it.

Eduard came later. When I saw him approach through the crowd, carrying his own bag and looking for us, I felt how much I had missed him. We did not pretend the months apart, or the months before them, had resolved what we argued about. We found somewhere to sit and ordered food. He listened to Zala describe the life she was building, asked her questions, and let her answer as an adult.

Luka sent us a photograph from home: two cups on a table, his girlfriend's hand resting beside one of them. It was the sort of glimpse he offered when he wanted us near without wanting us inside everything. I wrote back that I liked the picture. He sent a heart.

Nearly a year after she left Britain, in June 2022, Zala moved on to Colombia to teach English. She called us when she arrived. The room behind her was bare, and she was already talking about where she would go the next day. I heard excitement in her voice, and some uncertainty too. She was making a life; it did not have to be finished for me to trust that it was hers.

I thought of the title I had given those years in my notebook: *Locked-in freedom.* When we were confined, I had looked for a way out. Once we could travel, I found that distance alone did not free me from fear, love, or the wish to arrange other people's lives. The children went where they chose. Eduard and I made our choices and kept finding ways to meet each other afterwards. The sea was still there when I returned to it. I went into the water because I wanted to feel alive, and then I came home to the people whose lives I could never live for them.
