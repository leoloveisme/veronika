# Getting friends with cold

## Screenplay draft

TITLE: BANGKOK - DECEMBER 2006

INT./EXT. SUVARNABHUMI AIRPORT - DAY

The terminal doors open. A wave of hot air envelops Veronika. She stops under it while the crew continues with the bags, then follows them towards a taxi.

INT. BANGKOK HOTEL ROOM - NIGHT

Veronika calls home.

LUKA (ON PHONE)
Have you seen a train yet?

VERONIKA
Not yet.

ZALA (ON PHONE)
How hot is it?

VERONIKA
Like someone held a warm towel in front of the airport doors.

ZALA (ON PHONE)
Can you bring it back?

Veronika smiles.

MONTAGE - BANGKOK TO NORTHERN THAILAND

Veronika and the crew travel and film. She interviews people where they live, watches the camera settle into its frame, and checks the next deadline.

Each night, before opening the next day's schedule, she writes down one thing that surprised her.

EXT. NORTHERN THAILAND - NIGHT

The hills are dark. Veronika steps outside without a sweater and immediately draws her arms close to her body.

VERONIKA
I thought I came to the tropics.

CREW MEMBER
You did.

VERONIKA
Someone should tell the night.

She returns wearing a sweater and sits outside in the cool air.

VERONIKA (V.O.)
It was supposed to be warm in a tropical country. But December in northern Thailand brought back northern India.

FLASHES - NORTHERN INDIA, 1999

Veronika and baby Luka wrapped in warm clothes. Mountain air. Her hands pulling a coat closed.

BACK TO NORTHERN THAILAND

VERONIKA (V.O.) (CONT'D)
Perhaps I had a talent for travelling to warm countries at exactly the place and time they would prove me wrong.

She breathes and stops resisting the cold. It remains uncomfortable, but she stays.

MONTAGE - FOLLOWING DAYS

Veronika walks outside after dinner. On some mornings, she runs before breakfast. Afternoon heat returns; cool nights follow. She begins choosing the cold instead of waiting for comfort.

## Water

EXT. TRAIL IN NORTHERN THAILAND - MORNING

Filming in the hills is finished. Veronika follows a trail with a COLLEAGUE who has heard about a place to swim.

They reach clear water. Stones are visible below the surface.

The colleague takes off his shoes and steps into the shallows.

COLLEAGUE
It's freezing.

VERONIKA
Come back, then.

She laughs, but remains on the bank.

MEMORY FLASHES - CHILDHOOD LAKE

A CHILD'S foot loses the bottom. Water closes overhead. The sky disappears. An unseen hand pulls her upward.

BACK TO NORTHERN THAILAND

Veronika watches the surface. The old prediction that she will die by drowning echoes in her memory.

The colleague returns to the bank. There is no camera and no audience. Veronika could leave.

She removes her shoes.

Her first step into the water catches her breath. Her legs tense to retreat. She remains where she can stand securely, waiting until her breathing settles.

She moves farther. The colleague stays nearby without speaking. Veronika puts her hands into the water and splashes her face.

FLASH - THE CHILD'S LOST FOOTING. THE ABSENT SKY.

Veronika stands upright. The bank is beside her. She knows the way out.

She goes deep enough to swim a short distance, turns and returns. On land, shaking from the cold, she begins to laugh.

VERONIKA
Well, that didn't kill me.

Her colleague hands her a towel. Veronika dries herself and dresses. They walk back towards the road.

MONTAGE - SOUTHEAST ASIA

ON-SCREEN PLACE CARDS: LAOS. CAMBODIA. MALAYSIA. SINGAPORE. VIETNAM. BANGKOK.

Long filming days in Cambodia and Vietnam. Shorter stays in Malaysia and Singapore. During her reserved days, Veronika walks without the crew, reviews her notes and learns the names of people she interviewed too briefly.

She thinks of the cold water but does not search for another test.

INT. HOTEL BATHROOM - MORNING

At the end of a shower, Veronika turns the water cooler. She steps out of the stream almost immediately.

The following morning she does it again and remains for a few breaths longer.

INT. FAMILY HOME, BATHROOM - MORNING

Back home, Veronika finishes her first cold shower. A towel hangs over a chair. Eduard appears at the bathroom door.

EDUARD
Is something wrong with the hot water?

VERONIKA
No.

EDUARD
Then why were you shouting?

VERONIKA
I was making friends.

EDUARD
With whom?

VERONIKA
I'm not sure yet.

Eduard shakes his head, smiling, and goes to wake the children.

Veronika stays in the bathroom. She turns towards the shower, considering it again.

### Novel chapter - unchanged

```text
# Getting friends with cold

Bangkok, December 2006.

When the airport doors opened, warm air wrapped around me so thoroughly that I stopped for a moment beneath it. The crew went on with the bags. I followed, thinking I had chosen an excellent place to spend a winter.

We stayed in Bangkok overnight before travelling north. I called home from the hotel. Luka wanted to know whether I had seen a train yet; Zala wanted a description of the heat. I told her it felt as if someone had held a warm towel in front of the airport doors. She asked whether I could bring the towel back for her.

The next day we worked as we travelled. I listened to people speak about the places they lived, watched the camera find its frame, and tried to keep my own questions from becoming a list I repeated everywhere. I had promised myself before leaving Britain that I would pay attention. It took effort. A deadline could turn any journey into a sequence of arrivals, introductions, and departures. At night I wrote down one thing that had surprised me before I looked at the next day's plan.

By the time we reached the hills of northern Thailand, I had stopped carrying a sweater in my day bag. That evening I wanted it. The room was cooler than I expected, and outside the air made me draw my arms close to my body.

“I thought I came to the tropics,” I said to one of the crew.

“You did.”

“Someone should tell the night.”

I remembered northern India, where I had once been grateful we had packed clothes for a European autumn. I had expected warmth there too. Perhaps I had a talent for travelling to warm countries at exactly the place and time they would prove me wrong.

I put on my sweater and sat outside for a while. The cool air was uncomfortable only until I stopped arguing with it. I thought about how much of my life I spent arranging for the temperature, the room, and the day to suit me before I could enjoy any of them. I had learned in Spain to notice a breath without trying to improve it. I wondered whether I could meet cold in the same spirit.

For the rest of the trip, the thought stayed with me. There was plenty of work in the morning and enough heat by afternoon to make the cool night seem imaginary. Still, I looked forward to going out when the day had quieted. I began with a walk after dinner. On some mornings I ran before breakfast. Neither was an achievement worth telling anyone about. I liked the feeling of choosing to go outside rather than waiting until it was comfortable.

## Water

We had finished filming in the hills when I heard about a place to swim. In the morning I went to see it with a colleague who wanted to walk the trail. The water was clear enough to show stones beneath the surface. It was also much colder than I had expected.

I stood on the bank and remembered another stretch of water, years earlier, when I was a child. I had gone in and could not find my footing. For a moment the sky had disappeared. Someone pulled me out before that moment became the end of my life. I learned to swim afterwards, but whenever I approached open water I remembered how little my ability had mattered when I was frightened. An older woman's prediction that I would drown had stayed with me too, despite my disbelief in her power to know such a thing. // which chapter did we have it already in?

My colleague took off his shoes and stepped into the shallows. He called out that it was freezing. I laughed and told him to come back. Then I sat on a rock and watched him return. I had not brought a camera. There was no scene to capture and no audience to impress. I could leave without going in.

I took off my shoes. The first step made me catch my breath. My legs wanted to carry me straight back to the bank. I stayed where I could stand securely and waited until I could breathe without fighting for it. Then I moved a little farther. My colleague was nearby, watching without saying anything.

I put my hands in the water and splashed my face. For an instant the old fear arrived in full: the lost footing, the absent sky. I stood upright. The bank was beside me. I knew how to get out. I went in far enough to swim a short distance, turned, and came back.

I had imagined that courage would feel like a charge of energy. It felt, at first, like an argument with every part of my body. Once I was on land, I could not stop laughing. I was cold, and my legs were shaking, and I was still there.

“Well,” I said, “that didn't kill me.”

My colleague handed me a towel. He had no reason to understand why the sentence mattered, and I did not explain it then. I dried myself, dressed, and walked back with him. By the time we reached the road, I was warm again. The fear had not disappeared. I knew I would have to meet it another day if I wanted anything more from the experience.

Later we travelled on through Laos and other stops in the region. There were long working days in Cambodia and Vietnam, and shorter stays in Malaysia and Singapore before I returned through Bangkok. Each place deserved more time than our schedule gave it. I used some of the days I had kept for myself to go back over my notes, take walks without the crew, and learn the names of people I had spoken to too briefly. I thought of the water often, but I did not turn the rest of the trip into a search for another place to prove myself.

At a hotel one morning I turned the shower cooler at the end and stepped out of its stream almost immediately. The next morning I stayed a few breaths longer. It was a small, private experiment. I could stop whenever I wanted. I had spent years thinking of cold as something to endure until I could escape it. I was beginning to wonder whether I could get to know it instead.

When I reached home, Zala wanted the stories I had promised. Luka examined a photograph of a train before he looked at any of the other pictures. Eduard asked about the work, then noticed the towel I had hung over a chair after my first cold shower at home.

“Is something wrong with the hot water?” he asked.

“No.”

“Then why were you shouting?”

“I was making friends.”

“With whom?”

“I'm not sure yet.”

He shook his head, smiling, and went to wake the children for breakfast. I stayed in the bathroom a moment longer. Cold water had not answered the question I took to Thailand. It had given me one thing I could practise: meeting an unwelcome feeling, finding out what was actually happening, and choosing my next step while I was still there.
```
