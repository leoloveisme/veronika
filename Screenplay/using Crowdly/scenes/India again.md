TITLE: NEW DELHI - JANUARY 2025

INT. NEW DELHI AIRPORT - DAY

Veronika waits at the baggage carousel with a camera crew. MATEJ lifts the
camera bag and finds her staring towards the exit.

MATEJ Are you ready?

VERONIKA Ask me after I've slept.

They laugh and walk into the traffic outside.

Before the vehicle leaves, Veronika messages Eduard. He replies with a
photograph of bare Slovenian farmland crossed by a narrow line of trees.

INT. VEHICLE, NEW DELHI - DAY

The city moves around them. Veronika records an opening thought into her phone.

VERONIKA Auroville was founded with the promise of a community where money
would no longer govern a person's worth. What happens to that promise when
people have to grow food, repair buildings and pay for an ordinary day?

She stops the recording.

VERONIKA (CONT'D) Too certain.

MATEJ You haven't interviewed anyone yet.

VERONIKA Exactly.

MONTAGE - NEW DELHI AND NORTHERN INDIA

The crew interviews people, changes trains, drives long distances and eats
late. Veronika wakes in temporary rooms and briefly forgets which city lies
outside.

She knows instinctively where to charge her phone, where to find privacy for a
call, and how to leave before a room begins to feel permanent.

INTERCUT - INDIA / BRITAIN

Veronika calls Eduard. He sits over drawings for the Slovenian farm and turns
one towards the camera.

VERONIKA You've put in trees already.

EDUARD They take time.

VERONIKA So do films.

EDUARD I know.

He smiles, but he is tired. Veronika realises he has been doing most of the
work on a decision they made together.

VERONIKA Send me the plans. All of them, not only the photographs you like best.

INT. TRAIN - DAY

Six drawings wait on Veronika's screen. She studies them and writes questions
in the margins while the landscape passes.

EXT. INDIAN MARKET - DAY OFF

Veronika and Matej walk without the crew. After exhausting every subject about
the documentary, Matej asks about Eduard. Veronika asks about Ana.

They answer honestly enough to make the walk uncomfortable. Later they eat
together and discuss only the light moving across a wall.

EDUARD You sound as though you're making a film about us.

A FARM WORKER leads Veronika, Matej and the small crew along a path. She shows

Before filming, Veronika checks a message from Eduard: someone can provide more

ON PHONE: “We'll go when you're home.”

Veronika begins a reply. Matej asks for quiet. She puts the phone away.

The camera points towards a field. Matej steps backwards to widen the frame.

Veronika sees movement behind his left leg: a snake partly hidden by leaves.

VERONIKA Matej. Stop.

He freezes.

VERONIKA (CONT'D) There's a snake behind you. Don't move.

The worker sees it.

FARM WORKER Come away slowly.

Matej follows the instruction. Veronika sees how close he is and grabs a fallen

FARM WORKER (CONT'D) Veronika, leave it.

Veronika lowers the branch, trying to place it between Matej and the snake.

VERONIKA I'm all--

FARM WORKER We need medical help.

Matej is already calling. The camera lies where he placed it, its lens aimed at

The worker keeps Veronika still. Veronika looks at the discarded branch,
remembering the instruction she ignored.

VERONIKA Call Eduard.

MATEJ I will.

VERONIKA Tell him I know he's waiting for me.

Matej kneels beside her.

MATEJ You can tell him yourself.

Veronika looks at him, hoping he is right. Her unfinished message to Eduard
remains on the phone in her bag.

People who know what to do move urgently around her. Veronika stays still and
lets them work.

# India again


In January 2025, I arrived in New Delhi with a camera crew, a notebook, and the peculiar feeling of returning to a place that had continued living without waiting for me. The city did not owe me the woman I had been on my first visit. I was older now. I had children who could cross oceans without asking my permission, a husband who was looking at farmland in Slovenia, and a colleague beside me whose place in my life I still could not describe in a sentence.


Matej lifted the camera bag from the carousel and found me staring towards the doors.


“Are you ready?” he asked.


“Ask me after I’ve slept.”


We laughed and went outside into the traffic. I sent Eduard a message before we drove away. He sent back a photograph of a bare field we had visited together. A narrow line of trees crossed one side of it. He had begun to imagine where a house might stand.


The film had begun with a question about Auroville. Its founders had imagined a community where money would cease to govern the worth of a person. What happened to that promise when people had to grow food, repair buildings, welcome newcomers, and meet the cost of an ordinary day? I wanted to hear from people who believed in the place and people who found it difficult to live in. I wanted them to tell us what the question meant now, rather than ask them to act out an old dream for our cameras.


We did not go south at once. Our work took us through New Delhi and further into northern India. Between interviews there were train stations, long drives, meals eaten late, and mornings when I woke unsure which city lay beyond the curtains. Some days I was absorbed in the story. On others I noticed how quickly I could make a life out of temporary rooms. I knew where to charge my telephone, how to find a quiet corner for a call, how to be gone again before a place began to expect me.


Eduard asked about the work, and I asked about the land. At first we each answered too briefly. Then one evening I called while he was making a drawing for the farm. He turned the page towards the camera.


“You’ve put in trees already,” I said.


“They take time.”


“So do films.”


“I know.”


He smiled, but I heard the tiredness beneath it. We had agreed to look for a place together, and he was doing the looking while I travelled. I had not broken our agreement. I had also left much of its work in his hands. I asked him to send me the plans, not only the photographs he liked best. The next morning there were six drawings waiting for me. I studied them on a train and wrote questions in the margins.


Matej and I had learned to work beside each other again after everything we had told our partners. That did not mean the past had become simple. On a day off, we walked through a market and spoke about the film until there was nothing left to say about it. He asked how Eduard was. I asked after Ana. We answered honestly enough to make the conversation uncomfortable. Later we ate together and talked about the light on a wall. The four of us had made room for truths that still required care each time we touched them.


By the time we reached Chennai, the dry heat seemed to remain in my clothes after I stepped indoors. We travelled on to Auroville in May. The pace changed. Paths ran between trees and buildings; people stopped to speak as they carried out the work of a community that had been imagined long before many of them arrived. Each person we interviewed gave us a different account of what had survived from that vision.


One resident described the early hope that exchange within the community might be organised around contribution and need. Another showed us the records and payments that still had to be managed. Someone else said the most important part of the experiment was not whether a visitor saw cash changing hands, but whether people could learn to value work that earned little elsewhere. I kept all three answers in the notebook. A place could be founded on an ideal and still have bills, disagreements, and people who needed lunch.


The film was becoming more interesting than the version I had proposed. I had expected to ask whether Auroville had fulfilled its promise. I began to ask how anyone lived with a promise that could never be finished. The question followed me into a call with Eduard. He was at the coast, sorting through things we had accumulated in our house.


“You sound as though you’re making a film about us,” he said.


“Maybe a little.”


“I hope you give me a chance to answer.”


“You always have one.”


Afterwards I wondered whether that was true. I had listened to strangers for hours while Eduard’s desire for a home in Slovenia could still make me impatient in minutes.


We arranged to film at a farm serving the community. I wanted to understand the work behind the conversations about money and belonging: the people tending crops, the weather they could not negotiate with, the distance between a plan and a harvest. We asked where we could walk and what we could film. A worker led us along a path, stopping to show us the beds that had been planted and the ones being prepared.


That morning I had sent Eduard another message. He had found someone who could tell us more about the Slovenian land. *We’ll go when you’re home,* he wrote. I began to answer, then put the telephone away when Matej asked for quiet.


The camera was pointed towards a field. Matej took two steps backwards to widen the frame. I saw movement near the edge of the path behind his left leg: a snake close to the ground, partly hidden by leaves.


“Matej. Stop.”


He froze.


“There’s a snake behind you. Don’t move.”


Our guide turned at the sound of my voice and told us to come away slowly. I should have followed her instruction. Instead I looked at how near Matej stood and reached for a fallen branch, thinking I could put it between him and the animal. I had made quick decisions in difficult places before. For one stupid second that seemed like a reason to make another.


“Veronika, leave it,” the guide said.


The snake moved as I lowered the branch. I drew back, but not far enough. I felt a sudden blow against my left leg, sharp enough to make me look down before I understood what had happened. The branch slipped from my hand.


Matej came towards me. The guide called for him to stay clear of the snake and helped us move away from it. Someone took my arm and sat me down. I could see two small marks above my ankle. I wanted to say that I was all right, because that was what I usually said when people were frightened for me. The words felt premature even to me.


“We need medical help,” the guide said.


Matej already had his telephone out. The camera lay where he had set it, its lens facing the empty path. I watched him speak to someone, stop, and listen closely to the reply. His expression told me more than his words.


The worker who had shown us the farm stayed beside me. She told me to be still. I tried. My thoughts kept travelling back to the branch on the ground, to the instruction I had heard and ignored. I had wanted to pull someone I loved out of danger. Now he was kneeling near me, trying to arrange the help I needed.


“Call Eduard,” I said.


“I will.”


“Tell him I know he’s waiting for me.”


Matej looked up. “You can tell him yourself.”


I hoped he was right. In my bag, my telephone still held an unfinished answer about a field in Slovenia. Around us people moved with the urgency of those who knew what to do. I stayed where I was and let them work.
