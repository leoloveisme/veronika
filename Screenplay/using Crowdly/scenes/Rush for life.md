# Rush for life

The first thing I noticed was how everyone else became calm. At the farm, Matej spoke into his telephone with one hand pressed against his ear. The woman who had shown us the fields told me to keep my leg still. Another person went to meet the vehicle that would take us to a clinic. I wanted them to hurry. I wanted them to stop looking at the two marks above my ankle.

“I can walk,” I said.

“You’re staying there,” Matej answered.

It irritated me that he sounded like Eduard. I had once climbed a mountain in the cold and swum alone in an ocean. Those memories arrived as if they were evidence that my body would obey me now. Then I looked at my leg. It was beginning to swell, and I had no idea what had entered it.

The guide asked if I could describe the snake. I could give her its colour and where it had been. I could not tell her its name. She said that was all right; the people treating me would assess what was happening. She did not let anyone return to the path to find it.

I remember being lifted. I remember the ceiling of a vehicle and the sound of a door closing. Matej sat where I could see him. I asked for my telephone, wanting to finish the message to Eduard about the field in Slovenia. Matej told me he had called him.

“What did you say?”

“That a snake bit you. That we’re taking you to be seen.”

“Did you say I was all right?”

“I said you were talking to me.”

I wanted him to say more. He would not promise what he could not know. I turned my head towards the window. The road passed too quickly for me to hold on to any one thing outside it.

At the clinic, people asked questions I struggled to hear over the noise I was making inside my own head. They checked my breathing and the swelling and arranged for me to be taken on to a hospital that could treat a serious snakebite. I caught pieces of conversation: blood tests, antivenom, a call ahead. Someone removed the bracelet from my ankle and put it in my bag.

I had said I hated hospitals after Villette. I had said it so often that I thought it was a permanent part of me. Now I wanted a hospital with people who knew exactly what to do. I wanted the lights and the equipment and the ordinary competence of strangers. I wanted one of them to tell me I would be here tomorrow.

I tried to picture Eduard answering the call. Was he in his studio, looking at the drawings he had sent me? Had Matej reached him while he was outside among the trees? I hoped he had someone beside him. Then the ceiling above me blurred, and the thought would not stay in place.

That is where my own account runs out. Everything that follows came to me afterwards, from Eduard, Matej, the crew, and the records they carried home.

Matej stayed with me through the transfer. At the hospital, the team assessed the bite and treated the effects they could see developing. They gave antivenom and monitored me closely. The first hours did not yield the reassuring turn everyone wanted. My condition worsened, and I was moved into intensive care. Later, Eduard would ask whether the snake had been a viper, as the doctors suspected. No one on our crew had stood close enough to identify it with certainty. The important fact then was what the bite was doing to me.

Matej called Eduard again. This time he did not soften the words.

“She’s very ill,” he said. “I’m here. They’re treating her.”

Eduard asked him to repeat the name of the hospital. He wrote it down twice because his hand had begun to shake. Then he called our children. Luka offered to meet him at the airport. Zala called from Colombia and kept asking whether she should come to India or go to Slovenia. Eduard could only tell her what he knew: I was being cared for, he was going to me, and they would decide the rest as soon as there was something to decide.

Ana drove Eduard to the airport. They had made a place for each other in the complicated year before this one; now she helped him find his passport and checked that he had the hospital’s number. He said little on the drive. Before he went inside, she asked him to call when he arrived, even if there was no news.

By the time he reached my bed, I was unconscious. He took my hand and spoke to me anyway. He told me which flight he had taken and that he had forgotten to water the plants before leaving. He said Zala and Luka were safe. Then he told me about the field again: where the trees stood, where the light fell. The monitors answered for me in their own language.

Matej met him outside the room. He began to explain how I had seen the snake behind him and reached for a branch. Eduard stopped him.

“I know what happened,” he said. “You’ve told me.”

“She was trying to protect me.”

“I know that too.”

Neither of them could turn those facts into a reason why I should wake. They sat together for a while, two men who loved me in different ways, unable to do the one thing either of them wanted most.

Days acquired a pattern without becoming ordinary. A doctor described what had improved and what still worried the team. Eduard wrote down the answers so he could repeat them to the children without changing a word from fear. Matej helped our crew finish the practical work of leaving India; there would be no more filming. He came back to the hospital each day. Sometimes he and Eduard relieved each other. Sometimes they both stayed.

Zala arrived first, carrying a bag packed for a journey whose end she had not known when she began it. Luka came after her. In the hospital corridor they embraced without their old arguments about who had called whom more often. When they entered my room, Zala took the chair nearest my face and told me about a student in Colombia who had finally learned to pronounce a word she had been practising for weeks. Luka said my unfinished notebook was safe. They had no way of knowing whether I heard either of them. They spoke because I was there.

Eduard hated that all of them had to see me among tubes and machines. He had spent so long looking for a quieter place for us to live, somewhere we might sit outside and watch a tree grow. The hospital gave him no view he wanted to remember. Still, he learned the route from its entrance to my room and greeted the nurses who cared for me. He could not protect our family by refusing to be there.

As the days passed, the immediate danger changed shape. The medical team continued to treat and support me, but I did not wake. Eduard asked what it would take to bring me nearer home. The answer depended on my condition and on arrangements between hospitals, specialists and a transport team. Nothing about the journey was automatic. He knew we could pay for help that many families could not; he also learned that money could not make a person stable enough to travel.

When the doctors judged that a transfer could be planned, Eduard chose Ljubljana. He had spoken for years about returning to Slovenia. He had not imagined the first journey back would bring me there unconscious. The hospital arranged the handover with the team that would receive me. The crew helped pack the few things I had taken to India. Someone found the bracelet removed from my ankle and placed it in a pocket of my bag.

On the day we left, Matej stood beside the bed while the transport team prepared me. He said my name once. Eduard thanked him for staying. There was more to say between them than either could manage in that corridor. Matej stepped aside so Eduard could walk with me.

The aircraft carried us out of India in May. I did not know when we crossed a border or when the light outside changed. Eduard knew every time someone checked on me. He kept his hand near mine, careful not to interfere with the people working around us. At intervals he looked at the small bag by his feet, as if he could will its owner to ask for it.

In Ljubljana, another team was waiting. They took over without asking Eduard to tell our entire story at once. He followed them into the hospital and gave my name to the person at the desk. Years earlier, I had entered that city’s hospital world furious that my life had continued. This time no one could ask me what I wanted. Eduard sat down outside my room, placed the bag beside him, and waited for me to open my eyes.
