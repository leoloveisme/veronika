## Screenplay draft

TITLE: ALASKA - AUGUST 2010

INT. ALASKAN AIRPORT - DAY

Veronika arrives with a television crew. Before she collects her bag, a LOCAL PRODUCER approaches.

LOCAL PRODUCER
What do you think of America?

VERONIKA
I've been here for less than an hour.

He laughs. A moment later, with the camera ready, he asks again.

EXT. ALASKA - DAY

A landscape too large for the frame. Veronika delivers a rehearsed introduction to camera.

VERONIKA
From Alaska, we'll travel south across the United States--

She stops and looks beyond the lens.

VERONIKA (CONT'D)
Give me a moment.

The crew adjusts equipment. Light moves across the distance. Then Veronika turns back to the camera and continues.

INT. HOTEL ROOM - MORNING

Veronika calls home. It is evening for Eduard and the children.

LUKA (ON PHONE)
Are you going all the way to Texas on the ground?

VERONIKA
Some of it.

LUKA (ON PHONE)
That isn't the same thing.

VERONIKA
No. It isn't.

MONTAGE - THE ROUTE ACROSS AMERICA

A map fills with a route that refuses to form a straight line. The crew drives when the road matters and flies when the schedule wins.

Each arrival becomes a FLY-IN: the city name appears as Veronika reports to camera.

TITLE: SEATTLE, WASHINGTON

EXT. SEATTLE STREET / SHOP - DAY

Rain catches the crew between locations. They shelter inside while the CAMERA OPERATOR dries the equipment.

A WOMAN working in the shop asks what they are filming. Veronika gives the prepared explanation.

WOMAN
Have you spoken to anyone who lives in this neighbourhood?

Veronika looks at the schedule.

VERONIKA
Do you have time after your shift?

They rearrange the evening. Her interview replaces two planned scenes.

EXT. COMMUNITY GARDEN, CALIFORNIA - DAY

Vegetables grow in containers between buildings. Veronika approaches the CARETAKER.

VERONIKA
Could we film here?

Deal.

### Novel chapter - unchanged

```text
# Wild wild everything

Alaska, August 2010.

In Iceland I had gone into the ocean alone. A few weeks later I arrived in the United States with a crew, a route pinned across a map, and more people waiting for answers than I could keep straight. Before I had collected my bag, someone asked what I thought of America. I said I had been in the country for less than an hour. He laughed and asked again when the camera was ready.

The series was supposed to follow a journey from the north towards the south, although the places we wanted to reach refused to arrange themselves into a tidy line. We would fly when the distances defeated our schedule and drive when the road itself had something to tell us. I had argued for meeting people between the famous views. The producer agreed, then handed me a list long enough to make every meeting shorter than I wanted.

Alaska gave us more space than the lens could hold. I stood before the camera, delivered an introduction I had rehearsed, and then asked for a moment without speaking. Behind us the crew adjusted equipment. I watched light move across the distance and remembered that the ocean in Iceland had gone on without noticing me. Here too, the landscape had no need for my narration. We filmed it anyway. That was our job.

I called home before we left. It was morning for me and evening for Eduard and the children. Luka had begun a drawing he did not want to describe until I saw it. Zala described it in detail over his protests. I told them where I was. Luka found Alaska on his map, then wanted to know whether we would travel all the way to Texas on the ground.

“Some of it,” I said.

“That isn't the same thing.”

“No,” I admitted. “It isn't.”

In Seattle rain caught us between locations. We sheltered in a shop while our camera operator dried the equipment. A woman working there asked what we were filming. I told her the series title and began my usual explanation about routes and regions. She asked whether we had talked to anyone who lived in the neighbourhood. We had been there for half a day; our next flight was already booked. I asked if she had time to speak to us. She did, after her shift. We rearranged the evening. The interview was better than two planned scenes we later dropped.

Portland came next, then San Francisco and Los Angeles. Names appeared beneath my face on the screen as we filmed our introductions. I imagined the edited programme moving smoothly from one city to another. In life, I mostly remembered queues, misplaced cables, calls made from cars, and the sudden kindness of someone who stayed late so we could finish. I could speak easily to a stranger while the camera was on and then fail to ask the name of the person who carried our bags to the vehicle. I tried to correct that, though I did not always succeed.

In California I saw a small garden squeezed between buildings and stopped to look at it. Someone had planted vegetables in containers where there seemed barely room to stand. I asked the caretaker whether we could film. She said yes, provided we spent more time speaking with the people who kept it alive than photographing the plants. She was right. We stayed until the light changed. That night I sent the children a picture of a tomato growing beside a wall. Zala wrote back that ours at home were bigger.

We drove towards Las Vegas. Its lights were extraordinary, and so was the effort required to keep the machinery behind them out of the frame. After a day filming what visitors came to see, I went out without the crew and found myself watching people at work: cleaning, carrying, resetting each room for the next arrival. There was always another arrival. I thought of how often I had used the word *freedom* for my ability to leave a place, while someone else made it possible for me to arrive in it.

On a day away from filming we went to the Grand Canyon. The producer suggested a short piece to camera. I tried one and heard myself reaching for phrases that belonged to every large view I had ever described. I stopped. We took what footage we needed and put the camera away. I wrote to Eduard that evening: *I don't want to tell the children how big it is. I want them to see it someday and tell me what they notice.*

From the southwest we flew to Chicago, then east to Boston, New York, and Washington. The order made sense on a schedule and very little sense inside my body. I woke one morning unsure which city held the hotel room. In New York a viewer recognised me while I was buying food. She said she watched the cold-water documentary with her daughter. I was pleased, then surprised when she asked whether Luka and Zala travelled with me. I answered that they were at home with Eduard.

“They must miss you,” she said.

“I miss them too.”

She meant no harm. I carried the question back to my room. I knew where my children were, whom they would call if they needed help, what they liked to eat and which jokes made them laugh. I also knew that when they wanted to see me that week, they could switch on a screen more easily than they could reach me. I had accepted assignments because I loved the work and because our family could arrange itself around them. I had not asked often enough what those arrangements felt like to Luka and Zala.

I called Eduard. He was in his studio, speaking quietly because the children were asleep.

“Do they ask when I'm coming back?”

“Of course.”

“How often?”

“Veronika.”

“I want to know.”

He put something down. “Luka asks before you leave and then counts the days. Zala asks when she's angry. She also tells everyone what you're doing.”

I sat on the edge of the hotel bed. “That sounds like both of them.”

“It is.”

He did not turn their feelings into a judgment on my work. He had his own journeys with his paintings. We talked about dates, the promises I had made, and what I could still change. I moved one interview rather than miss the afternoon I had said I would call. It was a small correction. I wanted it to be the start of paying closer attention.

Washington gave us a day of formal buildings and people practiced in answering questions. In Florida the air felt heavy when we left the airport. By Texas I had lost the pleasure of announcing another arrival. We filmed the last scenes, thanked the people who had worked with us, and sent the equipment home. The camera operator asked whether I was ready to follow it.

“Not yet,” I said.

I had spent weeks moving through the United States faster than I could absorb it. Some encounters had stayed with me; others had already blurred. I was grateful for the work and tired of treating every departure as proof that I had been somewhere. I had planned to go on to Mexico when the assignment ended. Without a crew beside me, I could choose a slower pace or discover that I did not know how.

I called Eduard before crossing. We agreed on when I would call again. Luka wanted to know if I had seen a train worth drawing. Zala asked if Mexico would be on television too.

“Not this time,” I told her.

“Then how will we see where you are?”

“I'll send you a picture. And when I come home, I'll tell you.”

I put the telephone away and looked once more at the map. The line we had followed across America was impressive on paper. I had not yet decided what it meant. I took my bag and went south.
```
