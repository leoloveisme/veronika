# Roots and flames

On 6 February 2023, the first images arrived before I had finished breakfast. Buildings had fallen across southern Turkey and northern Syria. People stood in the cold where their homes had been, listening for voices beneath the concrete. I watched long enough to understand the scale of what had happened, then turned off the sound. The silence made it no smaller.

Matej called before noon. We had worked together for years, often with a camera between us and something neither of us knew how to explain yet. He asked if I thought we should go.

“I think we need to know who wants us there,” I said.

We spent the next days speaking with local contacts and aid workers, deciding what story we could responsibly make and where we could work without getting in anyone’s way. By the time we travelled through Istanbul towards Gaziantep, other people had been carrying the disaster for far longer than we had watched it. I reminded our small crew of that as we waited for our bags. A documentary did not make us rescuers.

I phoned Eduard from the airport. His birthday was still weeks away, but I had already missed enough days that I wanted to say it plainly.

“I promise I’ll be back for your birthday.”

“Come back before then, if you can,” he said.

I pictured him in the studio, one hand stained with paint, the telephone held between his shoulder and ear. When I said goodbye, I kept the promise in my mouth for a moment, as if saying it again would make the journey easier to contain.

On our first morning in the region, a woman showed us a row of keys she had taken from her pocket. She knew which door each key belonged to, though none of the doors stood where she had left them. We did not ask her to repeat the story for a better angle. Matej lowered the camera while she tried to separate one key from the others. Later, when she said we could film them, his hand was steady.

There were interviews we never used. There were places we did not enter. The work mattered to me, and I was never free of the uneasy thought that I could leave when we were done. Every evening I called home. Sometimes Eduard answered at once. Sometimes I heard the telephone ring while I looked at a clock and calculated whether he might be asleep.

“Tell me something you saw that wasn’t ruined,” he asked one night.

I told him about a man offering hot tea to strangers, though he had been waiting all day for news of his brother. Then I stopped. I did not want to turn that man into a lesson about hope for someone sitting safely at home. Eduard understood. We stayed on the line without speaking much.

When I returned, his new canvases leaned against the studio wall. A blackened field under a red sky. The shape of a house split into pieces, with a thin line of light between them. They were unlike the coast he had been painting before I left.

“Did I bring all this back with me?” I asked.

“Some of it,” he said. “Some was here already.”

He had listened to my reports, watched the news, and worked while I was away. I had imagined he stood still whenever I travelled. The paintings showed me how much could happen in a room I was not in.

Matej came to the house to review the footage. He knew the names behind the images and the silences around the interviews. We worked late, arguing over what belonged in the film and what had only moved us because we had been there. Eduard brought us food, spoke to Matej, and left us to it. I thought I knew the shape of our life: the work that took me away, the marriage that received me back, the friendships that crossed between them. Then the shape began to change.

It was not one sudden night I could blame on exhaustion. Matej and I began to look for each other when the work did not require it. We spoke about our families, then about the parts of ourselves we had kept out of those family stories. I knew he was married to Ana. He knew Eduard. We named those facts and went on speaking. Naming a boundary did not mean we had kept it.

The first time we slept together, I had already understood that I wanted to. Afterwards I sat on the edge of the bed and felt the plain weight of what I had done. Matej reached for my hand. I let him hold it, though neither of us could offer the other an innocent version of the evening.

We told our partners. I could not bear to return to Eduard’s table and ask him how his day had been while asking him, silently, to live inside a story he did not know. That was my reason. It did not make telling him generous.

He listened until I had finished. Then he asked, “Do you love him?”

“I care for him. I don’t know what that means yet.”

“You knew enough to sleep with him.”

“Yes.”

Eduard went into the studio. I heard him move a canvas and set it down again. I wanted him to come back and ask a question I could answer well. He had already asked the one that mattered, and I had not answered it well.

Ana called him some days later. They had known each other through our work and the occasional dinner; she was not arriving as a stranger carrying Matej’s version of events. They met for coffee, then talked again at the house while I was away. Eduard told me afterwards that she had come because he was one of the few people who understood exactly what had happened. I could hear the hurt in his voice even when he said her name kindly.

One afternoon Ana stayed. Eduard told me that too. She had come to him for comfort, and they had slept together. I looked at him and felt something hot and possessive rise in me before I had any right to it. I had wanted honesty from him without imagining how much it might cost to hear it.

“Was it because of me?” I asked.

“You are part of what happened,” he said. “But she and I made our own decision.”

I did not like that answer. It was fairer than the one I had been ready to give him.

Nothing settled quickly. There were nights when Eduard and I talked until we could no longer hear each other properly, and mornings when we made coffee and discussed the garden because neither of us could begin again before breakfast. Matej and Ana had their own conversations, which were not mine to describe. We stopped pretending that one confession had turned four people into a simple arrangement. When we met, there was tenderness, embarrassment, desire, and sometimes anger in the same room. We had to ask what each person wanted, and believe the answer might change.

Eduard’s birthday gave us a date to organise ourselves around. I had returned as promised. Luka came, and Zala travelled from Colombia for a visit. For one afternoon the house filled with the noise of our children arguing over who had remembered a family story correctly. Zala told us about her students and the city she had made her own. Luka said he was considering a move to the United States. He had not decided when. I watched Eduard take in the news, then ask Luka what drew him there. He listened longer than I might have.

At the table, Zala caught me recognising a place she mentioned in Spanish and leaned towards me.

“Mamá, you were there too, weren’t you?”

“Years ago. I don’t know it as you do.”

She grinned and began to tell me what had changed. Around us the others asked for translations, and we gave them. I had once enjoyed having a language that shut someone else out. That afternoon I wanted everyone at the table to hear her.

After they left, Eduard carried the plates to the kitchen. He waited until the water was running before he spoke.

“I have been looking at land in Slovenia.”

“For what?”

“A farm. A place we could make into a home.”

He showed me photographs later: a field, an old building, hills beyond it. He spoke about soil, trees, the light he remembered from childhood. I heard the wish beneath the practical details. Our children had lives elsewhere; he wanted somewhere to return to, and perhaps somewhere they could return to us.

“You want to move now?” I asked.

“I want us to start thinking about it.”

I knew how quickly my fear gave a settled place the shape of a cage. We had a home by the sea, and I could leave it for work whenever I chose. Why did another home frighten me? Perhaps because Eduard was asking me to imagine the years after the assignments, and I was not ready to set a date for them.

“I’m not ready to stop,” I said.

“I haven’t asked you to stop tomorrow.”

“But you’re asking me to see the end.”

He looked at the photographs on the table. “I’m asking whether you can see a place for me in what comes next.”

That hurt because I could. I had simply expected him to keep making room wherever I wanted to go. I told him I would visit the land with him. I could agree to buy it if we both loved it. I could not yet promise to live there. He said that was enough to begin with.

Over the following year we returned to our work and to each other without pretending either was easy. News of fires, floods and wars reached our table; some stories took me away again. Eduard painted. Sometimes Ana visited. Sometimes I saw Matej. The four of us were learning the difference between an agreement spoken once and the daily practice of living with it. There were days I felt close to all of them, and days I wanted the privacy of the woman I had been before any of this happened.

When a new documentary proposal brought India into view, I told Eduard before the plans were settled. I had been there before; he still remembered the fire in Goa. I saw it return to him as soon as I said the country’s name.

“You want to go again,” he said.

“I do.”

He did not try to forbid it. He asked where we would work, with whom, and for how long. I answered what I knew and admitted what I did not. Then he asked if we could look at the Slovenian land before I left.

“Yes,” I said. “Let’s go together.”

That night I found him in the garden, studying a space where he thought another tree might grow. He told me what kind he had in mind. I described a scene I hoped to film in India. We listened to each other until it was too dark to see the ground properly. I could not promise to become a person who stayed. He did not have to remain a person who waited without a wish of his own. In the morning we opened our calendars and made room for both journeys.
