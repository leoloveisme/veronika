## Screenplay draft

INT. FAMILY HOME, KITCHEN - DAY - 2004

Back from Spain, Veronika lays photographs across the table in order for Luka. Zala eats the orange she selected, guarding every segment. Eduard finds Veronika's breathing instructions among her notes.

EDUARD
Will you show me?

INT. FAMILY HOME - EVENING

Veronika and Eduard sit on the floor. She guides him through the breathing exercise. After a moment, he opens one eye.

EDUARD
Is it working?

VERONIKA
I don't know. You're the one doing it.

EDUARD
I was asking you.

They laugh and begin again.

MONTAGE - THE WORLD OPENS

News and conversations about Slovenia joining the European Union: work, travel, and the opportunities Luka and Zala may have when they grow up.

Veronika accepts television assignments. Passport stamps, trains, airports and hotel rooms. She returns with notebooks full of voices, food stains and unfinished questions.

At dinner, she tells stories too quickly. Zala interrupts with a story of her own. Luka waits for silence, then asks about the detail Veronika forgot to explain.

Between journeys, Veronika plants seeds in pots and watches for shoots. The telephone rings. A producer needs an answer. She leaves the watering can and hurries away.

INT. EDUARD'S STUDIO - DAY

Luka draws near Eduard while he paints. Zala rejects the spare brush and reaches for the one in Eduard's hand.

INT. CAR - DAY

Veronika drives Luka home from school.

VERONIKA
Your teacher said you talked about a programme you saw me present. Did you like it?

LUKA
You were there.

VERONIKA
I know.

LUKA
But you were here that day too.

Veronika understands. The programme was recorded weeks earlier. Luka is pleased by the trick of having two mothers: one on the screen and one at breakfast. Veronika smiles, then wonders whether she told him when she had gone away to film it.

MONTAGE - WORK AND FAMILY

Veronika films in another country while Eduard checks a school form and feels Luka's forehead. Someone helps with the children. A crowded calendar fills with journeys, exhibitions, appointments and promises.

Eduard sits quietly through dinner. After the children sleep, he talks to Veronika about a painting he hid away and later brought out again. She tells him about an interview that changed when the subject asked her a question. They remain talking long into the night.

INT. FAMILY HOME - NIGHT

Veronika leans against Eduard.

VERONIKA
Would you like us to move back to Slovenia?

EDUARD
Do you?

VERONIKA
I asked first.

He considers it.

EDUARD
Perhaps one day. I'd want to make a place where the children could stay as long as they liked.

Veronika imagines a house, Eduard's studio and soil she can plant. The thought of staying still unsettles her.

VERONIKA
Not yet.

EDUARD
I didn't say now.

She rests against him and lets the question wait.

INT. FAMILY HOME - EARLY MORNING

Before anyone wakes, Veronika practises the breathing exercise she learned in Spain. Her breath slows. Her eyes open and move to the calendar. She begins planning her next assignment.

MONTAGE - 2006

Veronika is now a familiar television presence. A producer hands her an invitation. She checks the dates before reading the subject.

At home, she feels a pain in her right breast. She waits. Days later, the pain remains.

INT. DOCTOR'S EXAMINATION ROOM - DAY

A DOCTOR examines Veronika, arranges tests and asks her to return when the results are available.

INT. DOCTOR'S OFFICE - DAY

Veronika sits opposite the doctor. The doctor explains what the tests have found and why a surgeon should see her.

DOCTOR
We have to consider the possibility of cancer.

The doctor's voice becomes indistinct. Veronika stares at her, absorbing almost nothing else.

VERONIKA
Could you write that down for me?

The doctor writes the next steps on a sheet of paper and gives it to her.

INT. MEDICAL BUILDING, CORRIDOR - DAY

Veronika catches her reflection in a dark window: coat buttoned, bag over her shoulder, outwardly unchanged. The paper is in her bag.

EXT. MEDICAL BUILDING - DAY

People pass on their way to ordinary appointments. Behind Veronika, the door opens and closes. She wants to call Eduard. She wants to walk until she finds another explanation for the pain.

She remains where she is, unable to take the first step home.

# The cage door is open at last

When I came home from Spain, Zala ate the orange she had chosen with great seriousness and refused to share even one piece. Luka wanted the photographs in order, so I laid them across the kitchen table and tried to remember which city came after which. Eduard found my page of breathing instructions among the notes and read it while the children argued over a picture of a train.

“Will you show me?” he asked.

I showed him that evening. He sat on the floor beside me, followed my directions for a few minutes, and opened one eye.

“Is it working?”

“I don't know. You're the one doing it.”

“I was asking you.”

We laughed and began again.

I had watched Slovenia join the European Union on a television in Madrid. At home, the news became part of ordinary conversation: who could work where, which journeys might become simpler, what opportunities the children would have when they grew up. No border had physically vanished when the broadcast ended. We still needed passports, tickets, money, and time, but for someone who had grown up with a map that could change under her feet, the idea of more open routes carried a force beyond paperwork.

I had already travelled far before 2004. India had taught me that getting somewhere did not mean I knew what to do once I arrived. Britain had taught me that I could make a home in a country I had once known mostly through its language. Spain had given me another practice to bring back with me. The world had been open in pieces all along. I could feel those pieces beginning to connect.

I wanted to see more of it. I said yes to assignments because the subject interested me, because someone had asked for my work, and sometimes because there was an empty week in the calendar and I hated to leave it empty. I came back with notebooks full of voices, food stains, and half-written questions. At home I told stories too quickly over dinner, while Zala interrupted to tell one of her own and Luka waited until we were all quiet before asking the thing I had forgotten to explain.

I planted wherever we stayed long enough for something to take root. A pot on a windowsill could occupy me as completely as a garden for an afternoon. I liked putting a seed into soil and accepting that I could not persuade it to hurry. Then the telephone rang, or a producer needed an answer, and I hurried away from it myself.

The children grew into their own routines. Luka liked to sit near Eduard while he worked, drawing on paper he had been allowed to use. Zala wanted the brush Eduard was holding, not the spare one he offered. When I was home, she pulled me into games whose rules changed as soon as I understood them. She could still exhaust me, but I no longer expected her to be quiet for my convenience. Her stubbornness was becoming a way of meeting the world.

Once, a teacher mentioned that Luka had talked about a programme he had seen me present. I asked him about it on the way home.

“Did you like it?”

“You were there,” he said.

“I know.”

“But you were here that day too.”

I took a moment to understand: the programme had been recorded weeks earlier. To him there had been two mothers, one on the screen and one at breakfast. He seemed pleased by the trick. I was pleased that he had noticed and uneasy that I could not remember whether I had told him I would be away when we filmed it.

Eduard and I could afford help. That mattered, and I tried not to speak as though I had solved a problem that money had made easier for me than for many mothers. Even with help, someone had to know which child had a fever, when school expected a form, and which promise I had made before agreeing to another trip. Often that someone was Eduard. We both loved our work. We both loved the children. Love did not arrange the calendar for us.

Some evenings he would be quiet through dinner and talk with me for an hour after the children were asleep. He told me about a painting he had put away because he could not bear to look at it, then brought out again because he could not stop thinking about it. I told him about an interview that had changed direction when the person I was speaking to asked me a question in return. We could be apart for days and still fall into those conversations as if neither of us had left.

One night I asked whether he would like us to move back to Slovenia.

“Do you?” he said.

“I asked first.”

He considered it. “Perhaps one day. I'd want to make a place where the children could stay as long as they liked.”

I pictured a house with space for his studio and soil I could plant. I wanted it. I also felt, almost at once, a fear that arriving there would mean I was expected to stop moving.

“Not yet,” I said.

“I didn't say now.”

I leaned against him and let the question rest.

I kept practising the breathing exercise from Spain. Some mornings I sat before anyone else woke. Other mornings I remembered it only when I was hurrying to catch a train. It helped me notice when I was anxious. It did not always tell me why. More often, I found myself using the quiet to plan the next thing.

By 2006 my work had become regular enough that other people could describe my career more confidently than I could. An invitation arrived and I looked at the dates before I looked at the subject. I told myself I was being practical. I had also begun to suspect that I was afraid of what I might think about if I stayed still for very long.

Then I felt pain in my right breast.

At first I waited for it to pass. When it didn't, I made an appointment. A doctor examined me, arranged tests, and asked me to return to discuss the results. I sat opposite her while she explained what they had found and why she wanted a surgeon to see me. She used the word *cancer* as a serious possibility. I heard it and then heard almost nothing else.

I asked her to write down what she had said. I needed to read it when I was capable of understanding her again. On the way out I caught sight of myself in a dark window: coat buttoned, bag on my shoulder, looking much as I had when I came in. I wanted to call Eduard. I wanted to walk until I could think of some other reason for the pain. I did neither for several minutes.

Outside, people passed me on their way to ordinary appointments. The door behind me opened and closed. I had spent two years thinking about how many places I was free to go. Standing there, I could not make myself take the first step home.
